Where elements come from: from the Big Bang to supernovae

Machine-assisted draft · native review pending

Hydrogen and helium are mainly from Big Bang, carbon to iron is mainly from Star fusion, and heavy element, such as gold and uranium, requires a neutron capture and concoction. The element periodic table is not a static list, but a UNiverse history.

Big Bang gave it to Lightness.

A few minutes after Big Bang, universsetemperature was so high that helium, helium and a small amount of lithium could be formed by proton and neutron. The heavier element is difficult to stabilize at this stage; the initial element list of unverse is very short. [2] [3]

Hydrogen accounts for about three quarters of ordinary mass and about one quarter of helium; this proportion is the result of a combination of universe science model and observation. It is not “all elements in place once”, but “light element, then star”. [2]

Dar: from hydrogen to iron

Star core concentrates hydrogen into helium, followed by helium fusion, carbon fusion, oxygen fusion, silica fusion at higher levels, generating heavier elements laterally until near the iron peak. [1][3]

Each of the iron-56s combines close to peaks and continues to aggregate without releasing energy but absorbing it. As a result, Big Massstar lost its support after the iron core was formed and its core collapsed, triggering a supernova outbreak. [1][5]

Carbon is a key node: three helium cores synthesize carbon through the tri-alpha process, jumping through the destabilizing bottlenecks of lithium, beryllium and boron. Without such a step, there would be no carbon-based chemistry and no subsequent oxygen, thorium or magnesium. [1][3]

Heavyer than iron: Neutron capture

Element above Iron Peaks cannot be produced efficiently with electric particle fusion because the cologne base increases with the charge. They were captured mainly by neutron: atom nuclear absorbs neutron, with beta decay, climbing down the isotope chain to a heavier element. [1][5]

The slow process (s-process) occurs in near-grave stars, timescale long; the fast process (r-proces) requires extremely high neutron fluxes, usually associated with a core collapse of supernova or neutron. Access to gold, platinum, uranium, etc. remains a common constraint between observation and Model. [1][5]

What do supernovas tell us?

The spectroscopy of the supernova remains shows that the explosion threw element synthesized internally from Star into the interstellar medium. These elementes were subsequently absorbed by the next generation of Stars and planets into a cycle. [1][5]

The phrase "we are made up of stardust" is literally based on carbon, oxygen, nitrogen, iron in humans, mostly from the nucleination inside Star; hydrogen comes mainly from Big Bang. But this is not “everyone comes from the same supernova”, but “element comes from multiple celestial events”. [3][5]

The abundance is not randomly distributed.

The abundance of element changes with the atomic number: light element is much greater, the odd effect is evident, there is a relative peak near the iron peak, and there is little overweight element. These models record the history of fusion and the evolution of Star. [3]

Observation abundance is cross-checked with star spectra, meteoriteiisotope and interstellar media spectra. The ratio of isotope to different sources distinguishes between r-process and s-process contributions. [3][4][5]

Three levels of reading elementource

First, look at element: light element, iron peak element, or overweight element. Second, look at the process: Big Bang, Star fusion, s-process, r-process, or laboratory synthesis. Third, look at the evidence: spectroscopy, meteorite or model. [1][3]

To separate the three layers, the element cycle table is no longer a memory sheet, but an astrophysic book. [2][5]

Iisotope and abundance

Different isotope from the same element, from different fusion paths. Sotope abnormality in meteorite, tracer of former solar system material, and isotope ratio in Star spectrum, distinguish between s-process and r-process. [3][5]

Solar system abundance is not “universe average abundance”. It is influenced by local molecular clouds, the history of star formation and subsequent evolution; the use of solar abundance to represent the whole of universe would ignore differences in the environment. [3]

Laboratory synthesis

Element, which is heavier than uranium, is mostly synthesized through a nuclear reaction in laboratories, with short life and production measured atom. They complement the top right corner of the periodic table, but do not explain the abundance of the middle-heavy element. [1][3]

The study of overweight element has added to “where does element come from” a feeder: the celestial process produces natural element and the laboratory process produces artificial element. Both lines work together to test nuclear stability and shell structure. [3][5]

After Iron Peak

Iron Peaks are not “heaviest element”, but “the highest part of each nuclear combination”. Over the iron peak, fusion absorbs energy; objects must rely on neutron capture, fission or co-generation to produce energy to continue to produce heavy element. [1][5]

That is why gold, platinum and uranium are much less abundant than iron: they have more few production paths, less frequent events and some products continue to be decay. Each of the peaks and valleys on the abundance scale is signed for nuclear physics and astrophysical events. [3][5]

The abundance scale is history.

Each of the peaks and valleys on the abundance scale is signed for nuclear physics and astrophysical events. Reading it is reading a compressed UNiverse history; only the name of eliment is missing the process behind it. [2] [3]
