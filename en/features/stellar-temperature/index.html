How to measure the temperature of a star

Machine-assisted draft · native review pending

Star surface temperature is not measured by temperature, but is inverted from the spectrum, brightness and radius. Colours are only clues, and really reliable are spectroscopy, effective temperature and light.

Distinguishing between surface and internal

Star's surface is usually referred to as** effective temperature**: a hypothetical bold text that has the same total radiation if it sends the same radius. The sun is about 5,772 K. [1][2]

The core of the Sun is about 15 million K, far above the surface. The core is the fusion zone, with the surface being the PV layer from which the photons eventually escape; the two are separated from the irradiated zone and cannot be described in the same temperature. [1][5]

Read from spectral

Star spectra has a line of absorption. The relative strength of hydrogen, helium and metal lines varies with the temperature: the hydrogen line is weak when too cold, and the hydrogen is easily ionized when too hot; a group spectrum is strongest near a given temperature. Comparing the observation spectrum with the Star atmosphere model can reverse the effectiveness of temperature. [3]

This is the physical basis of the spectrum classification O, B, A, F, G, K, M. The O-type surface can be over 30,000 K, the G-type is about 5,000-6,000 K, and the M-type is about 3,000 K; this classification is also used as the basis for NASA 's Star data. [3] [4]

Reverse from Brightness and Radius

Stacy Boltzmann's Law. [ Chuckles ] L=4 pi R^2 sigma T^4
IfL and radius ZXXQPH1QZX are known,T can be deciphered. The light is derived from distance and light, and the radius can be obtained from interferometry, binary or model; the mass, radius and light of the Sun can be cross-checked with the JPL parameter table in NASA. [1 [2][5][6]

And that's why "red" is not as simple as "cold": the red star's surface is less than the sun, but the radius is large and the total light can be much higher; the white dwarf's surface is hot, the radius is small and the total light is low. [1][3]

Colour & & Red

Star colours generally vary with temperature: blue and white, orange red and cold. But interstellar dust causes the blue light to decline more, making Star look red; it's called red, not cooling. [1]

So only rough filters can be used in colours to estimate temperature. Accurate temperature is formulated by combination of spectral, photometric and radiuss, with the deduction of dust polishing, accompanying star contamination and instrument response. [3]

Mass-light relation of the main sequence star

The light of the main sequence star rises with mass and approximates an empirical relationship withL ∝ M³·⁵. The larger the mass, the higher the core temperature and pressure, the faster the fusion rate, the higher the surface. [2] [3]

But this relationship is not established for all Stars. Red giants, white dwarfs, Neutrons have different structures and energy sources; putting the main sequence on them, they get the wrong temperature. [1][2]

Three traps when reading temperature

First, the term “effective temperature” is used as “temperature at every point on the surface”; the surface of star is characterized by an imbalance of temperature, star spots and convective fluids. Second, use the word "colour" as the word "effective"; redification and atmospheric model change results. Thirdly, the “core temperature” is mixed with the “overture”; the two are three order of magnitude. [1][3]

When you see a star's temperature, you ask: is it a valid temperature, a colorful temperature, or a core temperature? Is it spectrometry, light or model? These three answers may be completely different. [2] [3]

Where's the radius?

Effective temperature depends on the radius. The radius may be derived from the geometry of the two stars, the agular diameter of interferometry, or a combination of light and distance. Double-eating gives the most direct radius, but few samples; interferometry is suitable for near-neighbored stars; model relies on star structural assumptions. [1][2]

If the radius is large, pressL=4π R²σ T⁴, temperature will be underestimated. In turn, error in distance estimates changes lightness and changes temperature. So "temperature" was never measured alone, and it was tied to distance, radius, light. [1][2]

Effective temperature Error

Spectra-composed errors come from atom data, atmospheric models, microflows and instrument responses; different teams may give dozens of K to hundreds of K differences to the same star. For a close neighbour like the Sun, the pressure can be small; for a remote star, the system is even larger. [2] [3]

When reading temperature, do not read only centre value. Look at the error strip, look at the method, see if it cross-checks with the radius, the light. A temperature without error is not usually more precise, but information is omitted. [1][3]

The sun as a ruler.

The sun's effectiveness is 5,772 K, often used as a reference for a G-type star. It is not a temperature of “averagestar”, but a recent, most easily measurable sample. [1][2]

Put other Stars next to the sun to understand the position of "hot" versus "cold": O-type stars can heat more than five times, M-type stars can cool half; red superstar surfaces are colder than the sun, but light is much higher. [2] [3]

Temperature is not bright

The brightness of a star depends on the degree of light and distance, and temperature only describes radiation in a unit area of the surface. Red superstars can be much colder than the sun, but brighter because the radius is large; white dwarfs can be hotter than the sun, but darker because the radius is small. The brightness is the most common confusion when reading Star data. [1][3]

Sun

1.989×10³⁰ kg, or 1 solar mass, of the Sun's total mass, is often used as a conversion unit for the entire mass ladder. The values are proposed by the International Astronomical Union for nominal solarmass parameters GM☉ divided by the gravitational constant G conversion, relative to uncertainty approximately 1×10⁻⁴.
