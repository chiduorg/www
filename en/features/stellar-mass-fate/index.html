Stellar mass and fate: who becomes a white dwarf, who a black hole

Machine-assisted draft · native review pending

Star's mass determines core temperature, fusion rate and lifetime, and generally determines the end point: white dwarf, neutron or black hole. But mass losses, double star and metal abundances can move around.

Mass decides fate.

Star relies on gravitational binding plasma and on core fusion to generate pressure. The larger the mass, the higher the core temperature and density, the greater the fusion rate, the higher the power in units; this makes it brighter and the fuel burn faster. [1][2] Mass is not the only variable, but it is the first variable. Initial mass, metal abundance, rotation, double-star mass transfer and magnetic field all alter the evolutionary path; saying “how many times the sunmass must turn into a black hole” is a slogan that crushes complex processes. [2][5]

Life of main sequence

Hydrogen concentrates into helium in the main sequence. Life expectancy is roughly M^-2.5: 10 sunmass stars with a lifetime of only tens of millions of years order of magnitude; half sunmass red dwarfs can burn hundreds or even hundreds of millions of years. [1][4] The sun is about 10 billion years of primary sequence life, and now it's about half. It will expand into a red giant in the future, and the outer layer will be blown away, leaving a white dwarf; it is not an “explosion”, but a slow loss of the outer layer. [2] [4]

Three endpoints.

Low massstar leaves a white dwarf, supported by Electron, with a ceiling of about 1.4 sunmass (Chandraseka limit). Medium mass core collapses form a neutron star, supported by a simple neutron with pressure and force interaction. [4] [5] The core collapse of Big Massstar can form a black hole. The event vision is not a “hole”, but a bundary from which light cannot escape; its radius is proportional to mass, but its density decreases rapidly with the radius. [5] [6]

Mass Loss and Double Star

Big massstar often loses a lot of mass with a star wind, and the actual end is determined by “how much is left” rather than “how much was born”. The mass of the two stars moves, co-packs and merges to deliver two Stars to areas that cannot be reached by single-star models. [2][5] Supernova eruptions are not the only ending. Some large massstars may collapse directly, and some white dwarfs may trigger Ia supernovas after they accumulate in two stars; there are multiple channels behind the same sentence “star is dead”. [5] [6]

What do you call it?

The two-star orbit is the most direct weight: the Kepler law gives the mass, the hyphenation gives the mass. Radial speed, shelter, microgravity lenses and star-shocking each provide independent restraints; for isolated stars, mass tends to rely on model and spectroscopy extrapolations. [1][5] Mass and radius. Only mass, no radius, cannot distinguish between white dwarfs and neutrons; only radius, no mass, and no simple and pressurized type. [5] [6]

I'm sorry.

The Sunmass is a convenient unit, not an average of all Stars. The initial mass function indicates that most Stars are smaller than the Sun and that a few large massstars contribute most radiation and weight to element. [2][5] Writing Star's fate into three fixed orbits would ignore mass losses and twin stars; describing a black hole as a “universe vacuum cleaner” would ignore an orbit that could still be stablely bypassed outside the event horizon. It's clear that scene doesn't become a myth. [6]

In-depth reading: Initial mass function

The initial mass function describes how a group of stars were distributed at birth: the number of low massstars is high and the number of high massstars is low. It affects the evolution of the stars, supernovas and element yields; when discussing “typical star”, it must first indicate whether it is quantitative or photometric. [2][5]

Deep reading: Metal abundance and star wind

Metal abundance changes opacity and starwind intensity. Large massstars with low metal abundance may lose less mass and eventually retain a larger core; high metal abundancestars may be blown off the outer layer by a star wind. So the same initial mass can end differently. [2][5]

Deep reading: Two Star Channels

Two-star mass shifts, co-pack evolution and convergence can produce objects that are difficult to form by single-star models, such as double-white dwarfs, X-ray twin stars and double black holes. The gravitational wave event from observation is one of the direct evidence of the twin-star passage. [5] [6]

Read deep: Observation evidence

Starmass can be estimated through two-star orbits, gravitational lenses, star-shocking and spectrospectral models; end-point identity is determined by light, radius, spectral line and explosive remains. To equate “mass large” directly with “black holes” would ignore the interaction between mass losses and twin stars. [1][5]

Continue: double-star mass shift

The mass transfer in the two stars changes the mass and orbit of two stars; the common layer phase may combine the two stars. Many special celestial bodies (X-ray twin stars, double pulsation stars) can only be interpreted through two-star channels. [5] [6]

Continue: Starwind losses

Large massstar often loses mass with a star wind rate associated with metal abundance, lightness and rotation; the actual end is dependent on the remaining mass, not the birth mass. Make the initial mass directly at the end of the line. [2][5]

Continue: Metal abundance

Metal abundance affects opacity and starwind, as well as the evolution trajectory of Star. Low metal abundance is likely to be tighter and less loss mass; high metal abundance is likely to be lost earlier. [2][5]

Continue: Observation constraints

Two-star orbits, velocities, sheltering, astrometrics and interferometry give a mass or radius; different methods are cross-checked. A single spectra model gives mass, error could be big. [1][5]

Continue: soundary presentation

Thresholds such as “8 times sunmass” are near boundary, not switches; rotation, double stars, metallic abundance and mass losses move baseary. Cops should write “about” and “depending on”. [5] [6]

Something to look back on.

Star fate is determined by the initial mass, mass loss, metal abundance, rotation and twin stars. The main sequence life is roughly scaled by M^-2.5; low mass ends with a white dwarf, medium mass core collapses to neutron, and large mass cores can become black holes. 8 Thresholds such as solarmass are close to division, not switches. Double-star mass shifts, co-packs and combinations produce single-star model hard to interpret objects. The term mass is to be cross-checked with two-star orbits, gravitational lenses, star seismology and spectra model; only mass, with no radius, cannot identify the endpoint. [1][5][6]

Mass / Stars & compact objects

Sun

1.989×10³⁰ kg, or 1 solar mass, of the Sun's total mass, is often used as a conversion unit for the entire mass ladder. The values are proposed by the International Astronomical Union for nominal solarmass parameters GM☉ divided by the gravitational constant G conversion, relative to uncertainty approximately 1×10⁻⁴.

Betelgeuse: 3.28×10³¹ kg, a red supergiant known only to order of magnitude

3.28×10³¹ kg, approximately between 16.5 and 19 sunmass. It does not have measurable orbital companions, and the values are entirely reliant on evolutionary models and can only be given to order of magnitude, which is about 30 per cent relative to uncertainty.

Temperature / Stars & cosmic extremes

Solar core temperature

The core of the Sun is approximately 15 million K, much higher than the visible surface of the Sun. Core fusion is turned into solar energy; this value describes the internal area and cannot be confused with the lightball's effectiveness temperature.

Space / Stars & interstellar

Stellar-Mass Black Hole Event Horizon

The event radius of the star class black hole is given by the Swazi radius, which is positive for mass; 10 times the solar mass corresponds to approximately 2.95×10⁴ metres, or about 30 kilometres. This number is a radius, not a diameter.

Supernova Remnant

Supernova remains are fragments that swelled upwards after the Big Massstar explosion and luminous crust formations that sweep through the interstellar medium. Sampled by a crab nebula, it's about 10 light-years, about 9.5×10¹⁶ metres in diameter.
