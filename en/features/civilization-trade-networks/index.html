Civilizations and trade networks: the Silk Road was not one road

Machine-assisted draft · native review pending

The Silk Road, the Indian Ocean monsoon route, the Trans-Sahara caravan and the Passage, together constitute a network of links to the former modern world. Trades, religion, technology and disease flow along the network, and the Empire is only one node.

It's not a road.

The Silk Road is a 19th century European name for a cross-Eurasian trade route. It includes land oases, savannah corridors, Indian Ocean sea lanes and regional markets, with no single endpoint and no year-to-year road. [1] Businessmen tend to travel only for one period and the goods are diverted in multiple places.

Commodities and perceptions

Silk, ceramics, spices, gold, salt, horses and slaves are common commodities; religion, writing, art styles, technology and disease are also transmitted along the network. Buddhism has been introduced from India into Central Asia and China, and Islam has spread along commercial routes, with black deaths moving along Euro-Asian trade and military forces.

Indian Ocean and Sahara

The Indian Ocean monsoons have created regular routes between Arab, Indian, South-East Asian and Chinese ports; trans-Saharan merchants have connected West African gold and salt to North Africa and the Mediterranean. [3] These networks are more stable, more day-to-day than the single narrative of the Silk Road and are often ignored in the history books of the European Centre.

Empire and intermediaries

The Mongolian Empire, the Ottoman Empire, Safavi, the Mughal and the Malian Empire provide protection, taxation and markets at different points. The empire was not a precondition for trade, but sometimes an obstacle; businessmen, fleets, oasiscity and religious networks were the persistent connectors. [1][3]

Common misunderstandings

The common misconception is that “the Silk Road only transports luxury goods”. Salt, food, cloths, horses and everyday tools also flow in large quantities; luxury goods are only the easiest to record. [2] Another misunderstanding is that “globalization began in Columbus”; the former modern transcontinental network exists, albeit at different speeds, sizes and participants.

Goods and transit

Silk, china, spices, gold, salt, horses, slaves and cloths move around the network; traders often travel only for one period, and goods change repeatedly in oasis, ports and markets. [1] Trade is not a line “from China to Rome”, but a network of multiple nodes and intermediaries.

Religion and disease

Buddhism has been brought into China from India through Central Asia, Islam has expanded along commercial routes and empires, and Christianity has developed different traditions in Armenia, Ethiopia and Europe. [2] The movement of black and dead diseases along Euro-Asian trade and military forces indicates that the network also transports pathogens.

Empire and Businessman

The Mongolian Empire provides station and protection, Ottoman competes with Safavi for business, Mughal and Mali control regional markets, and the Empire collects taxes and invests in roads and city. [3] Businessmen, flotillas, oasiscity and religious networks are persistent connectors and empires are only one node.

The former modern globalization

The speed, size and participation of former modern networks are limited and cannot be equated with modern globalization; however, “Columbia had no intercontinental links before” and was neither established. [1][3] Trade networks are fragmented, regionalized and restructured, not a straight line of continuous expansion.

Currency and credit

Long-distance trade gives rise to money orders, credit and partnership systems; businessmen use trust networks, religious communities and family relationships to reduce risk. [1] Not all transactions are cash-based, and credit instruments allow inter-temporal and cross-border transactions.

Ports and monsoons

Indian Ocean trade relies on monsoon cycles, with ports city providing ship repair, warehousing, supply and legal arbitration; and the Red Sea, Persian Gulf and East African ports competing. [2] The monsoon writes the table on the trade network.

Network fracture.

Empire collapses, plagues, climate disasters and pirates cut off routes, and traders reroute or retreat to regional markets. [3] Trade networks are not permanent expansion, but prosperity, fragmentation and restructuring.

Key figures

  • The Silk Road, as a term of reference, emerged only in the nineteenth century, not as a single path.
  • Zhang Zhang made the West Territories about 138 B.C.
  • Papermaking was spread to Central and West Asia after the war on Roth in 751.
  • Black death arrived in Sicily in 1347 and then swept Europe.
  • In 1498, Dhaka arrived in India to change the Indian Ocean trade pattern.
  • Timbuktu was the West African Academic and Trade Centre in the 14th-16th century.
  • Indian Ocean trade depends on monsoons, and the duration of the voyage is wind-oriented.
  • Former modern trade was mostly in the middle of a single-way journey.

Quick check.

  • Monsoon: Changing wind systems with seasons, affecting sailboat duration.
  • Oasis City: Supply and trading nodes on desert merchant roads.
  • Transit trade: goods are traded repeatedly at multiple nodes.
  • Bills of exchange: credit instruments for payment abroad.
  • Dynasty: The network of trade and diplomacy is wrapped in political ceremonies.

A common reverse example.

The Silk Road is not a road from Chang An to Rome. It is a network of commercial routes, oasis, ports and intermediaries; goods are transferred many times, and traders rarely travel fully. A single line of the network could be lost to the local market.

Keep checking the directions.

The Indian Ocean shipwreck archaeology, Dominique, Islamic geographic literature and African trade city archaeology can be found. The movement of goods, money and letters is used to rebuild networks rather than merely chart routes.

Self-test question three.

  1. ** Why not the Silk Road? ** It is a network of commercial routes, oasis, ports and intermediaries, with multiple transfers of goods.
  2. ** How does monsoon affect trade? ** The sailboat sails on a monsoon cycle and the port and the voyage period are written to the time table.
  3. ** What breaks the network? ** The collapse of the empire, the plague, the climate disaster and the pirates cut off routes, and the diversion or regionalization of businessmen.

If there's only one thing to remember,

The history of trade networks is not a road map, but a history of people, goods, religion, disease and credit moving together.

Put the numbers back in scale.

A modern truck would take weeks from Changan to Rome; it would take months to a year for the former modern merchant team to complete the same distance and would change hands several times. The speed differentials shape prices and risks.

Six time anchors.

  • ** Trans-Saharan trade: gold, salt and merchant teams of 8-16th century**: around 800 A.D., approximately 1,226 t 13.8 billion years ago. Trans-Saharan trade peaked in the 8th to 16th century, when the camel merchants transported West Africa gold, salt, cloth and books to North Africa, linking the Mediterranean to West Asia; it was not a single route. [4]
  • ** The Malian Empire: Gold and Salt Roads of about 1235**: About 1235 A.D. The Malian Empire lasted from about 1235 to the sixteenth century, with the Niger River, gold and trans-Saharan salt road at its core; Timbuktu and Gao became important trade and academic city in West Africa. [5]
  • ** The Mongolian Empire: 1206 Eurasia Corridor**: 1206 A.D. · 820 · 13.8 billion years today. The Mongolian Empire was founded in 1206 and subsequently expanded into one of the successive land empires in history; it also brought about war, disease and demographic restructuring by linking Europe with Europe through kiosks, commerce and local administration. [6]
  • ** The Ottoman Empire: Transcontinental Order 1299-1922**: about 1299 A.D. 727 t 13.8 billion years ago. The Ottoman Empire, which expanded from a small territory in 1299 to a trans-Eurasian empire, invaded Constantinople in 1453 and was abolished in 1922; its governance is based on a complex multi-religious, multi-legal structure. [7]
  • ** The Mughal Empire: South Asian palaces in 1526: about 1526 A.D. * About 500 years from today * 13.8 billion years. The Mughal Empire was established in 1526, and the Akba period was extended to large parts of South Asia, known for its Persian-language palace culture, Mansabdal system and Taj Mahal; it ended in 1857. [8]
  • ** The Safavi Empire: 1501 Isfahan and Shia State Church**: 1501 A.D., approximately 525 t 13.8 billion years. The Safavi Empire was founded in 1501 to establish the Shia 12-Imam as the State religion, and the trade between city and silk was reshaped in the first era of the world, with Isfahan at its centre. [9]

Connect the anchor to a line.

Six time anchorages spread from the 8th century to the 16th century, covering West Africa, steppes, West Asia and South Asia. They remind us that trade networks are not a route from Europe, but that there are many regional centres that bite each other through ports, oasis, monsoons and swirl stations. [4][5][6][7][8][9]

The year was considered a cyber node, not a dynasty: 800 years of trans-Saharan trade had matured, 1235 the Malian Empire controlled the gold and salt roads, 1206 the Mongolian Empire opened the savannah channel, 1299 Osman connected the Balkans to Anatolia, 1526 the Mughals and 1501 Safawi redistributed the flow of goods between the Indian Ocean and the Persian Gulf. [4][5][6][7][8][9] The network will break down and will be rebuilt by bypassing the fracture.
