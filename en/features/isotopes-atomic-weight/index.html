Iisotopes and atomic weight: why carbon is not exactly 12

Machine-assisted draft · native review pending

Carbon is not 12.000, but about 12.011. Natural carbon is a mixture of carbon-12, carbon-13, carbon-14, with a weighted average of isotope abundance; the mass of a single atom remains the integer.

No, atomic number wasn't atomic night.

Element by the number of protons definition: carbon has six protons, forever carbon. Atomic weight also contains neutron: Carbon-12 with 6 protons, 6 neutrons, Carbon-13 with 7 neutrons and Carbon-14 with 8 neutrons. [2]

The number of mass in a single atom is almost integer, as Proton is close to the mass in Neutron and electron mass can be ignored. However, “element” atomic weight is the weighted average of natural isotope mixtures, so decimals occur. [1][2]

Why is carbon 12.011?

Of the natural carbon, about 98.9 per cent are carbon-12, about 1.1 per cent carbon-13, and carbon-14 trace. The weighted average was slightly above 12, receiving approximately 12.011. [2]

This figure is not “carbonatom's mass”, but “average atomic mass of natural carbon”. With different sources of carbon, the isotope ratio may vary slightly, so the IUPAC sometimes gives toomic weight a space rather than a fixed value. [2]

Standard weight is a compartment.

Many of the eliment ' s atomic weights are expressed on an inter-zonal basis, as isotope is made up of different geological, biological or industrial samples. IUPAC's standard atomic weight table will mark "between" or "single value" and specify the applicable conditions. [2] [3]

Carbon-12 has been designated as 12 atomic mass units, which is the historical ruler; it was later replaced by Moor and Avogardro constants, but “carbon-12 = 12 u” remains a common reference. [1][2]

How do you measure the mass spectrometry?

The mass spectra separates the sample from the mass ratio and detects the relative abundance of each iisotope and mass. A high-resolution mass spectra can distinguish between isotope and molecular debris, where the mass is small. [3][5]

Modern measurement, based on mass spectrometry and calibration standards; isotope abundance prevents distillation during sample preparation. Results from different laboratories and methods vary in decimal places. [2] [3]

Radioactiveiisotope

Carbon-14 half-life for approximately 5,700 years for archaeological fixed years and uranium-238 half-life for approximately 4.47 billion years for geological fixed years. They are not part of the “atomic weight” but rather are manifestations of isotope stability. [4]

The chemical properties of iisotope are almost identical and mass differences affect diffusion, evaporation and metabolism. Iisotope effects are both tools and interferences in geological, climate and biological studies. [3] [4]

Read the three differences between the two.

First, the distinction between atomic number, mass and atomic weight: proton number, nucleus number, weighted average is not the same. Second, distinguish ** single atom** from ** natural sample**: the former is approximately integer, the latter with decimals. Third, distinguish between ** standard value** and ** sample value**: Standard weight is reference and actual samples may fall within range. [1 [2][3]

These three layers are separated to understand why the figures on the periodic tables are not integers or random rounding. [2][5]

Iisotope versus geology

Iisotope is geology and climate fingerprints. The ratio of carbon-13 to carbon-12 can reflect the organic cycle; the ratio of uranium-238 to lead-206 can be fixed in years; and the ratio of oxygen-18 to oxygen-16 can be inferred from the ancient tymperature. [3] [4]

These applications depend on the premise that the iiiisotopepepope ratio in the sample was recorded at the time of formation and was not significantly modified thereafter. Corrosion, weather and biological processes can all be rewrited, so sampling is as important as pre-treatment and instruments. [3] [4]

The history of the atomic weight

Dalton used hydrogen = 1 as the baseline, later changed to oxygen = 16, then carbon-12 = 12. Each time the benchmark was changed to bring tomic weight closer to physics while preserving the ease of chemical calculation. [1][2]

Modern tomic weight no longer relies on a single physical standard, but rather on a constant between Moore and Avogadero. However, “carbon-12 = 12 u” remains a common reference because it connects atomic mass units directly to nuclidesmas. [1][2]

Why not an integer?

Atomic weight is the weighted average of natural isotope mixtures, not the mass of individual atoms. The isotope ratio varies slightly from sample to sample from source, so standard atomic weight is sometimes given to compartments. [1][2]

And that's why "carbonlight 12.011" and "carbon-12 mass 12" can be set up simultaneously: a description element, a description nuclide. [2] [3]

This is why standard needs space. When reading it, ask what kind of sample it describes. [1][3]

The standard value is not the end point, but the best current reference; new samples, new measurement will continue to modify it. [2] [3]

Reading atomic weather is a tradition of continuous caliber. [2]
