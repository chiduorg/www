The light-year: a ruler for distance, not a duration

Machine-assisted draft · native review pending

Light-year is the distance light travels in a vacuum in one year, about 9.4607×10¹⁵ metres. It swapped the astronomical distance for “how long is the light going”, but it is still a unit of length; it sees millions of galaxy outside the light-year, and it sees its past millions of years ago.

What's light-year?

Light-year is a unit of length. Definition is the distance from which light spreads along a straight line in a vacuum. Julian takes 365.25 days and the speed of light takes 299,792,458 m/s, multiplying them by approximately 9.4607×10¹⁵ metres, or 946 trillion km. [1][2]

There are two easy-to-neglect words in this definition: vacuum and Julian year. Light slows in the medium, but light-year uses a vacuum light speed; Julian year is the fixed age used in astronomical calculations and is not equal to the number of days per calendar year. The more precise the definition, the more likely it will be to reproduce. [1][4]

Why light-year

Write interstellar distances in metres or kilometres and the numbers quickly lose their intuition. The nearest star is about 4.24 light-years, or approximately 4.0×10¹⁶ metres, and the diameter of the Milky Way is about 100,000 light-years, or 9.46×10¹⁷ km. light-year compresses a long string of zeros into comparable scales. [2] [3]

It also links distance to light speed. The light from the celestial body is transmitted at a limited speed, and the further away it is, the longer it is transmitted. So the "light-year" also tells us "how long the light is gone," but the two statements cannot be replaced: one is length and one is Time. [1][4]

Look back at Time.

When we say we see a neighboring star outside 4.24 light-years, we see a light from 4.24 years ago. Seeing 2.5 million light-year-year-olds of Galaxy, seeing it 2.5 million years ago. The greater the distance, the earlier the history is seen, it is not a visual technique, but a result of the limited speed of light. [3] [4]

It also means that astronomical images are not "shows of the moment." A remote star that explodes today, and we have to wait a long time to see it; we have seen a supernova outbreak that may have occurred a long time ago. Astronomy naturally carries time retroactivity, but retroactivity is determined by distance and light speed, not by the will of the observation. [3]

4.24 light-years

Neighboring stars are the closest Star to the Sun, approximately 4.24 light-years. It takes tens of thousands of years to fly at the speed of the Traveler; it takes 4.24 years to fly at the speed of light. This contrast: light-year wrote about the air between Stars on the order of magnitude. [3][5]

However, 4.24 light-years is not “small”. It's about 4.0×10¹⁶ meters, about 268,000 original unit. The orbit of Neptune in the solar system has an average radius of about 30 astromical units, nearly 9,000 times the distance of neighbouring stars. It's an intergenerational journey, as seen by the artificial detectors. [1][5]

The Milky Way and Fairy.

The surface diameter of the Milky Way system is often summarized as about 100,000 light-years, and the solar system is located near the Orion's arm at about 26,000 light-years. The Galaxy is about 2.5 million miles away, one of the longest-sized galaxy visible to the naked eye. These figures are placed on the same scale so that the hierarchy between “star” and “galaxy” can be seen. [2] [3]

These values themselves vary: the diameter of the galaxy depends on the inclusion of dark matter faintness, satellite galaxy and curved acoustics; and the distance of the Fairy is dependent on the caliber of the father-made star, the red giant star and other scales. light-year units are precise and distance values are still uncertainty. [3][5]

Light-year is not year

The most common misreading is the use of the light-year unit. "This star 100 light-years" means distance, not "100 years ago." If “100 years of walking”, that's time; light-year in numerical terms equals a year of walking by light, but the gauge is length. [1][2]

The second misread is to read "see 100 light-years" as "see a UNiverse 100 years ago." Strictly speaking, what is seen is the light from the object 100 years ago, and how the “then” at the celestial body is compared to the “now” on Earth and depends on the simultaneous learning of the terminologies. The day-to-day presentation can be simplified, and the physical presentation needs to be distinguished. [4] [5]

Scale steps

From Earth to Moon about 1.3 light seconds, to the Sun about 8.3 centimeters, to Neptune about 4.2 light. And beyond that, the neighbouring star is 4.24 light-years, the star pack is about 444 light-years, the center of the Milky Way is about 26 million light-year, the Concubine is about 2.5 million light-year, and the radius of observation UNiverse is about 93 billion light-year. Each level is not a unit, but a reference system. [1][3][5]

Light-year is appropriate for the distance between Star and Galaxy and for the inner distance of the solar system. Interplanetary missions are more natural with astromonic unit or light; writing “Moon is 1.3 light seconds” is easier to read than writing “4×10⁻⁸ light-year”. The selection of units is itself a readable design. [1][4]

Common misunderstandings

The first misunderstanding is that “light-year can converse adulthood”. Can't. It shares only one year of light with the year, not a scale. The second misunderstanding is “the larger the light-year, the older the celestial body”. The distance is age-related, but the object's own age is different; a 10-billion-year-oldstar can only be a few hundred times-year-old. [2] [3]

The third misunderstanding is that it is now. All long distances have phototransmission delays, but they are too small to be ignored in daily scale. The fourth misconception is that “light-year numbers are the exact values of light-transmissiontime”. In universe science scale, a distinction is also made between phototransmission time, inherent distance and co-mobilization distance. [4] [5]

How to read astronomical numbers?

When you see the light-year number, you do three things: replace it with metres or astronomical unit, confirm the order of magnitude; then look at what layer of structure it corresponds to (interplanetary, interplanetary, intergalaxy); and finally ask what the range method of this value is. light-year unit does not itself have arror from distance and model. [1][3]

Light-year is a compression tool for astronomical writing and is not a substitute for physical concepts. It turns the length of the "light walk" into a carryable ruler, but it requires readers to remember that the distance on the ruler is the distance, and that the light spreads to thetime. Dissociate the two so that the astronomical numbers don't become science fiction dates. [2] [4]
