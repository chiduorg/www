Wet-bulb temperature: why it can be more dangerous than air temperature

Machine-assisted draft · native review pending

Wet ball temperature synthesizes a number of temperature and humidity. 35 °C The wet ball is close to the physical limit of the human body to evaporate the heat; the higher the temperature, the harder it is to excrete the heat.

What's a wet ball?

Wet ball temperature is the temperature that is read when a thermometer wrapped in wet cloth is balanced in air. The water evaporates, the air is dryer, the evaporated faster, the wet ball is lower; the wetter the air, the slower the evaporation, the wet ball is nearer the temperature. [2]

At 100% of the relative humidity, the wet ball is equal to the temperature; when the relative humidity is low, the wet ball is much lower than the temperature. It is not a physiometric index, but a clear amount of physics. [2][6]

Why?

The body evaporates by sweat. The core rises when the environment is wet and nears the skin temperature (approximately 35°C), and the sweat can hardly evaporate. [1]

35 °C is often referred to as the “theoretical survival cap”, but is not a precise death threshold. Wind speed, metabolism rate, clothing, age, health status and behaviour change the real risk; in the cold, wind and water, people can endure longer. [1][2]

Wetball and heat are not the same thing.

Thermal index (heat index) is the synthesis of temperature and relative humidity as a “body experience” for public warning; the wet ball is the amount of termodynamics to assess evapotranspiration capacity. The two are relevant but cannot be replaced by one another. [2]

The same temperature, 35 °C, may be 20 °C when the wet ball is 20 %; 90 % when the wet ball is close to 34 °C. The latter has much greater physical stress. [1][2]

How? How?

Wet ball temperature can be measured directly by the wet ball temperature or by the dry ball temperature, relative humidity and air pressure. The calculation requires the humidity and pressure of anaccuracy; the result is significantly altered by the relative humidity of anaccuracy. [2][6]

In the climate study, wet ball temperature is often calculated by mode-out temperature and humidity fields. Different extreme values are given for different reanalysis data, different urban heat island processing. [1]

Why is it more dangerous than temperature?

The temperature only tells the environment how hot it is, and the wet ball tells the body whether it can disperse. In a high-wet environment, the temperature appears to be “only 33 °C”, but the wet ball may be close to 35 °C, sweating and spreading near failure. [1][2]

Urban heat island, higher risk when nighttime heat and humidity are added. No cooling was possible at night, and no window was restored to the body; a few days of high-wet ball events were more dangerous than a single-day peak. [1]

Three questions at the time of early warning.

First, is this "35°C" temperature or wet ball? Second, is the humidity relative, the humidity or the dot? Thirdly, are early warning intended for healthy adults, or are they older persons, children, outdoor workers? [2][6]

Answering these three questions clearly would make it possible to move from a weather adjective to an operational physical risk. [1][2]

Physical limit is not a line.

35 °C Wetball is a theoretical reference and is not the threshold for death for all. Healthy adults are able to endure longer times in conditions of coolness, wind, water and low metabolism; older persons, children, pregnant women, outdoor workers and cardiovascular patients are at higher risk. [1][2]

The evaporation efficiency of sweat fluid is also affected by wind speed, clothing, solar radiation and skin area. The same wet ball temperature, standing in front of a fan and lying in a windless room with a different feeling of physical stress. [2]

City and night

City concrete and asphalt heat during the day, heat at night and form a heat island; at night temperature in high-humid areas is limited and the body does not recover windows. The public health impact of successive high-wet ball events is often greater than the single-day peak. [1]

Early warning systems usually consider temperature, humidity and sustainability in conjunction with population vulnerability. A combination of high humidity, low wind speed and high night temperatures is missing from the “ceiling temperature” alone. [1][2]

Read "35°C" first

Wet ball, temperature, heat index? Is it an instant peak, or does it last for hours? Outside or indoors? These three distinctions determine whether 35 °C is "uncomfortable" or "dangerous." [2][6]

Wet ball and spot

The spot is the temperature when the air cools to saturation; the wet ball is the temperature when the evaporation cools to balance. Both reflect humidity, but physical processes differ: dots see condensation, wet balls see evaporation. [2][6]

At 100% of the relative humidity, temperatures, spots are close to the wet ball temperature; while the air is dry, spots can be well below the wet ball and the wet ball below the temperature. When reading meteorological data, do not use the three as synonyms. [2]

Wet ball temperature needs humidity and air pressure.

The dots need only a vapour content, and the wet ball needs a balance between air pressure and evaporation. Treating the spot as a wet ball overestimates or underestimates the evapotranspiration of heat; treating the heat index as a wet ball mistaking the public warning body perception indicator as the amount of termodynamics. [2][6]

Separate the two volumes so that early warning can be read correctly. [2][6]

Read the alerts, first the dry ball, the wet ball and the spot.

Temperature and clinical readings

Clinical thermometers are calculated using kelvin; NIST and BIPM's thermometers give both conversion and recurrence methods. [4][5] Differences between body temperature and method can be found in the MedlinePlus description. [3]

Human body temperature

Approximately 37 °C (310.15 K) is a commonly used body temperature reference value rather than a precise temperature that every person has every hour. Temperature readings vary according to factors such as the individual, time period and measurement.

Boiling water

In the vicinity of standard atmospheric pressure, water boils at about 100 °C (373.15 K). Boiling point changes with pressure: lower pressure boils with lower pressure and higher pressure with higher pressure.
