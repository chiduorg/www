The habitable temperature window: 0–100 °C is only a start

Machine-assisted draft · native review pending

Liquid water exists at about 0-100 °C below 1 bar, but is more than subject to tymperature. Pressure, humidity, radiation, chemistry and timescale cut the window.

0-100 °C, not the whole picture.

Under 1 bar, pure water boils near 0 °C and 100 °C; this area is often used as a “liquid water window”. But the pressure changes, the boiling point changes, and the ice point changes; the water on the plateau is boiling at less than 100 °C, and the high pressure on the deep ocean keeps the water liquid in a wider range of temperature. [5] [6] So "0-100°C" is a condition, not a UNiverse constant. To judge whether an environment is fit to live, at least the following questions must be asked: the pressure, whether the water is pure or salt water, and whether there are radiation and oxidants. [1][5]

Water, pressure and phase

The water phase divides the solid, liquid, gaseous state into regions, and the baseary is determined by the temperature and pressure. Under three phase pressure, liquid water is not stable; beyond the threshold, the liquid gas interface disappears and conventional “boiling” becomes meaningless. [5] [6] This explains why the liveable belt cannot draw only one temperature line. The size of the planet, mass, atmospheric thickness and clouds alter surface pressure and greenhouse effects and move the actual window of liquid water. [1][2]

Wet ball and heat risk

For the human body, the dry ball is not all. The sweat is difficult to evaporate in a high-wet environment, and when the wet ball is close to 35 °C, even under shadow and in front of a fan, life can be endangered within hours; at the same temperature, the dry environment is much safer. [3] The cold is similarly complex: the wind takes the watch, the cold index separates the “temperature” from the “body feeling”, and the wet clothing accelerates the temperature loss. The liveable window must be written at the same time as temperature, humidity, wind and clothing. [3]

Window of an outer planet

The habitable belt of an outer planet normally estimates the “range of distance that allows the presence of liquid water” by the brightness of the star with the orbit of the planet. It is a geometric/energy starting point and does not contain atmospheric composition, magnetic field, tidal locking and cloud feedback. [1][2] The surface of Venus is about 464 °C, with a pressure of about 90 times that of the Earth; the average temperature on Mars is as low as dozens of degree Celsius, and the pressure is too small. All of them are near or on the edge of the “star fit belt”, but they are not a habitable sample, suggesting that a single temperature indicator will fail. [2] [4]

How?

The surface temperature can be replayed by thermal radiation spectrum, infrared imaging and occultation; atmospheric temperature profile by spectral absorption line; and planetary balance temperature by star brightness and albedo. Each with a model hypothesis cannot be read directly as " 25°C somewhere". [2][5] In laboratories, phase, heat, evaporation and heat conductivity can be accurate; moving laboratory data to planet scale must indicate pressure, composition and time average. [5] [6]

I'm sorry.

“Livability” is not the same as “life”, nor is it the “human being able to live naked”. It indicates, at best, that liquid water and critical chemical reactions are likely to be sustained under given models. [1][3] Writing the temperature window as a single zone conceals the effects of pressure, humidity and radiation; writing “discovery of liveable planets” as “discovery of life” crosses the chain of evidence. Both beaming. [2] [3]

Deep reading: atmosphere and albedo

The planetary balance is determined by star brightness, orbital distance and albedo; large-temperature chamber effects lift the surface, and clouds and ice may reflect back more sunlight. Planets in the same orbit can vary dozens or even hundreds of degree Celsius on the surface due to differences in the atmosphere. [1][2]

In-depth reading: Oceans and health capacity

Liquid water is larger than water capacity, and the oceans absorb and redistribute large amounts of heat; and the monsoon, ocean current and evaporation of the continent, unlike the sea ' s warming rhythm, alter local typerature windows. Dwellingability is not a single number for a planet, but a long-term count of the climate system. [3][5]

Read deep: timescale

Short-term moderation is not equivalent to long-term habitability. Orbital variations, star brightening, geological activity and atmospheric flight change the environment in millions to billions of years; the “window” must be judged to indicate the average and how long it lasts. [2] [3]

In-depth reading: indicator baseary

The average surface temperature, the maximum temperature, the wet ball temperature answer different questions. To replace the whole with an indicator, the word “occupyingly moderate” is wrongly written as “long-term habitability” and the word “habitatable belt” from the model output is wrongly written as “discovered life” across the chain of evidence. [1][3]

Continue: Wetball indicator

The wet ball temperature combines temperature with humidity and is closer to human dissipation; the dry ball temperature only describes the air temperature. In hot and humid areas, wet ball indicators are more warning of risks than maximum temperatures. [3]

Continue: Pressure conditions

The boiling point of the water drops with pressure, and less than 100 °C on the plateau boils; the opposite is the case in the high-pressure environment. 1 standard atmospheric pressure must be filled when “water boils at 100°C”. [5] [6]

Continue: Cloud and feedback

Clouds reflect the sun and absorb infrared radiation; clouds, cloud heights and the size of cloud drops change the planet ' s net energy balance. Cloudfeedback in climatemodel is one of the main uncertainties in habitability judgements. [1][2]

Continue: ocean heat inertia

Ocean-heat capacity is high and temperature rises and cools are slow; it buffers short-term fluctuations and transports water to high latitudes. The temperature window of a planet cannot be judged solely by the top balance of the atmosphere. [3][5]

Continue: indicator

Average temperature, maximum temperature, wet ball temperature, ocean heat content and seasonal length answer different questions. The replacement of the whole with an indicator would lead to the misstatement of “frequent moderation” as “long-term habitability”. [1][3]

Something to look back on.

The liveable window is not a temperature line, but rather the temperature, pressure, humidity, radiation, chemistry and timescale. 1 bar 0–100 °C is a laboratory reference; highlands, deep sea, Venus and Mars indicate pressure and atmosphere rewrite windows. For humans, the wet ball is closer to the heat risk than the dry ball is; for planets, clouds feedback and ocean health determine long-term statistics. When referring to “habitability”, it is important to indicate whether liquid water is possible, humans are viable or life has been discovered, with a completely different level of evidence. [1 [2][3][5]

Temperature / Room temperature

Boiling water

In the vicinity of standard atmospheric pressure, water boils at about 100 °C (373.15 K). Boiling point changes with pressure: lower pressure boils with lower pressure and higher pressure with higher pressure.

Temperature / Mid temperature

Venus mean surface-temperature reference

The NASA Venus fact sheet lists average surfaces as 737 K, about 464 °C, with surface air pressure about 92 bar. It is the average of the planet scale and does not correspond to the top of the cloud temperature, nor does it represent a complete unity of location and moment.

Temperature / Low temperature

Mars mean surface temperature reference

The information on the Earth's near-Earth surface average is approximately 210 K (-63 °C) and the NASA information table gives the average atmospheric average about 214 K (-59 °C). 208.15 K (-65 °C) on the temperature axis is the round value of the overview information; it is neither measured at a particular landing point nor measured by day or by season.
