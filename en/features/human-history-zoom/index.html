Why human history disappears on the cosmic timeline

Machine-assisted draft · native review pending

Putting 13.8 billion years into a year, less than two seconds for 5,000 civilizations, less than half a second for the industrial revolution, and only a few milliseconds for the Internet age. It's not human history too short, it's linear time axes that crush short events into pixels.

Let's start with Human History.

“Human history” can mean at least four different horizons: intellectuals as a species for about 300,000 years; agriculture for about 12,000 years; text and civilization for about 5,000 years; industrial revolution for about 250 years; and Internet penetration for about 30 years. [2][3][4]

These four horizons are not equidistant scales on the same line, but are different events on four levels. Put them together so that there is an instinct that “human history is short”; each layer has its own rhythm. [4]

What will happen to a year?

One year, one year, 13.8 billion years, one day, 37.8 million years, one hour, 15.8 million years, one minute, 2.6 million years, one second, 438. [2]

At this rate, wise people appear at around 8:40 p.m. on 31 December; agriculture at about 2 hours and 45 minutes; text and civilization at about 11 minutes; industrial revolution at about 34 seconds; and Internet penetration at around 2 seconds. [2][3][4]

It's not a precise calendar, it's an order of magnitude. It says:** It's not that human history has no content, but that the universime axis is too thick, crushing the last segment to the edge. **

Why isn't the linear axis enough?

The linear time axis is suitable for the big event “From Big Bang to today”, but not for human history. To make 5,000-year-old civilizations visible on the 13.8 billion-year axis, it is necessary to magnify the axis twice as much as10⁷; when it is amplified, the early universe becomes a line. [2]

A logarithmic axis can accommodate both, but at the expense of a “map of distance”: the number of times between the adjacent scales is the same, and the visual “single segment” may represent a significant time differential. When reading a logarithmic chart, you must read the scale, not the length. [2] [4]

Where does human life go?

The global average life expectancy is about 73 years, with wide variations across regions. [1] Place 73 years into 13.8 billion years, on a ratio of about5×10⁻⁷; 5,000 years of civilization, about 1.5 per cent; 250 years of industrial age, about 29 per cent. [1][2]

The same life expectancy can be “near invisible” in different reference systems or “mostly half”. That's the power of the scale reference: not the numbers have changed, but the denominator has changed. [1][4]

Civilization continuation

“Civilization 5,000 years” usually refers to a documented society, not to “humans begin to live in groups” or “the presence of wise people”. Agriculture occurs earlier, city and country later; the starting points are different from one region to another. [3][4][5]

Using “civilization” as a unifying starting point would ignore the time tables of the Mesopotamiya, Nile, Indus, Yellow River, Andes, etc. 5,000 years is a rough comparison, not a single switch for all mankind. [4] [5]

The right way to read "disappear."

Human history “disappears” on the universe axis, not because it is irrelevant, but because the particle size of the axis is inappropriate. In other words, it fills the picture immediately: 5,000 years in one day, the industrial revolution in about 72 minutes, the Internet in about 8.6 minutes. [2]

So when you see "the human being on the universiteime axis is only a moment," you ask, "How long is this moment?" What's the axis? Who's the denominator? Answering those three questions clearly would not be considered as a measration. [2] [4]

One day, one hour, one minute.

One hour, about 1.58 million years, one minute, about 260,000 years, one second, about 438. The wise man has 300,000 years of about 11 minutes; civilization 5,000 years of about 11 seconds; the industrial revolution 250 years of about 0.57 seconds; and the Internet 30 years of about 0.07 seconds. [2]

This group is even more extreme than the one year pressure: the same event can change from 11 minutes to 0.07 seconds at different denominators. It's not the event. It's the scale. [2] [4]

When reading the time axis, look at the unit spacing and then the duration of the event. If the unit interval is longer than the event, the event can only be shown as one point; this is not a “short event”, but a “simplistic ruler”. [2] [4]

Selection of reference systems

The same human history, which was almost invisible for 4.5 billion years on Earth, was dominated by civilization for 5,000 years, and was completely different in a person's 73 years of life. The reference system is not neutral, it determines what is seen. [1 [2][4]

The walls of the museum often magnify human history in isolation, as the relationship between agriculture, city and the State is not clear without magnification; the time axis of the unverse studies must crush human history to the edge, because the main line is galaxy andstar. Both approaches are correct, but the problem is different. [2] [4]

Origins of Agriculture

Early Industrialisation in Britain

The United Kingdom experienced continuous changes in production, energy and transport from the second half of the eighteenth to the first half of the nineteenth century. The year 1760 is the time point for navigation, not the date on which all countries enter the industrial age simultaneously.
