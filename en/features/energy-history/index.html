A history of energy: fire, steam, electricity and the next transition

Machine-assisted draft · native review pending

Energy conversion is not a straight line of “new energy instead of old energy”. Firewood, coal, oil, natural gas, nuclear energy and renewable energy have been superimposed over a long period of time, with every conversion reshaping labour, city and geopolitics.

Fire and agriculture

The control of fires allows humans to cook, heat and change landscapes; agriculture transforms solar energy into food and changes the surface through population growth and land reclamation. [1] These early energy conversions do not have a “fuel market”, but they provide the basis for convergence with empires.

Coal and steam

The 18th century steam engine converted the chemical energy of coal into mechanical energy, allowing plants to move away from hydro and wind locations. High coal density and transportability contribute to railways, iron and steelization. [2] The industrial revolution is not a single invention, but a combination of energy, machinery, capital and labour.

Oil and electricity

At the end of the nineteenth century, oil provided high-energy-density liquid fuels for internal combustion engines and electricity delivered energy to households and factories. Together, they shape the motor, aviation, petrochemical and city grid. [1] [3] The core of energy conversion is not just “fuel”, but also transport, storage and end-of-pipe equipment.

Nuclear and renewable energy

Nuclear fission entered the grid in the middle of the 20th century, providing a high-density base charge, but with safety, waste and problems of regulation. Wind, photovoltaic and battery costs have declined in the twenty-first century, but intermittentity, energy storage and grid modification have become new bottlenecks. [2] [3]

Why is the conversion slow?

Energy systems have long lifespans, infrastructure is expensive and benefits are stable. Firewood, coal, oil and natural gas have long coexisted; Fossil fuel still accounts for about 80 per cent of global primary energy today. [3] “Transformation” is more like a combination of superseding and substitution than a one-night switch.

Common misunderstandings

The common misconception is that “the energy problem is solved by the invention of a new technology”. Energy conversion requires simultaneous changes in power generation, transmission, storage, end equipment and power; energy density, material supply and grid stability are all hard constraints. [1][2] Another misconception is that “renewable energy is not contaminated”; manufacturing, land and minerals still have environmental costs.

Energy density and EROI

The energy density of coal, oil and uranium is high, facilitating transport and centralized use; the energy density of firewood and biomass is low and requires large tracts of land. [1] EROI (return on energy investment) measures “how much energy is spent”, depending on the quality of the resource and the manner in which it is exploited.

Electricity grid and intermittent

Wind and voltage power varies with weather, and capacity factors are usually lower than fire and nuclear power; storage, demand response, cross-regional transmission and flexible power are required. [2] The grid is not a “plug in” pipe, but a system that requires real time balancing frequencies with voltage.

Why is conversion superimposed?

Energy conversion is usually not the disappearance of old energy sources, but a new form of superseding: coal and firewood, oil and coal, and natural gas and renewables. [3] Today, Fossil fuel still accounts for about 80 per cent of global primary energy and electricity for about 20 per cent of final energy.

Social conditions

Energy conversion also depends on capital, land, labour, war and policy. Steam machines require coal and iron, electrification requires grids and standards, and photovoltaics require silicon, silver, glass and reversers. [1] [3] Technically feasible does not mean that the system is deployable.

Energy poverty and equity

Hundreds of millions of people around the globe still lack access to electricity and billions depend on traditional biomass; energy conversion is not only a technical curve but also a distribution problem. [1] Energy needs for charging, cooking, heating and transportation determine whether the transition is acceptable.

Nuclear energy and waste

Nuclear fission energy density is extremely high and low-carbon, but construction costs, accident risks and long-lived waste require institutional arrangements. [2] Nuclear fusion is still in the experimental phase and cannot be precalculated as “unlimited clean energy”.

Efficiency rebound

Increased energy efficiency reduces unit service costs and may increase total consumption, called reborn effect. [3] Energy efficiency is therefore often associated with price, standards, land use and behavioural change.

Key figures

  • Fossil fuel still accounts for about 80 per cent of global primary energy.
  • Electricity accounts for about 20 per cent of the final energy source.
  • Coal, oil and natural gas combined continue to dominate power generation and transport.
  • The capacity factors for photovoltaic and wind power are generally lower than those for fire and nuclear power.
  • Lithium ion cell energy density is approximately 150-250 wh/kg.
  • Hundreds of millions of people globally remain without electricity.
  • Nuclear fission energy density is much higher than Fossil fuel.
  • Energy conversion usually takes place over 10 to decades.

Quick check.

  • Primary energy: from natural sources such as coal, wind, sun.
  • Terminal energy: energy used by end-users, e.g., electricity, gasoline.
  • EROI: return on energy investment.
  • Capacity factor: Ratio of actual capacity to rated capacity of power generation equipment.
  • Energy storage: Energy is stored for future use.

A common reverse example.

Renewable energy is not “automatic zeromission”. The manufacture of photovoltaic panels, wind machines and batteries requires minerals, energy and transport; the life cycle is low, but not zero. The comparison of energy sources is based on a combination of soundary and timescale.

IEA Energy Statistics, IPCC Energy Chapter, EROI Research and Network Flexibility Report. Look at the difference between “end-of-pipe energy” and “primary energy”.

Self-test question three.

  1. ** What's the difference between primary and end energy? ** Primary energy is a form of natural direct supply; end-use energy is end-use electricity, gasoline, heat.
  2. What is it? ** Rate of return on energy investment: ratio of energy obtained to energy invested.
  3. ** Why is energy conversion superimposed? ** Old energy sources will not disappear at once and new forms will gradually increase their share; infrastructure will determine the speed of the system.

If there's only one thing to remember,

The energy history is not a “fuel replacement table”, but a history of changes in energy density, infrastructure, capital and social systems.

Put the numbers back in scale.

One kilogram of coal is about 24-30 MJ, one kilogram of oil is about 42-46 MJ and one kilogram of uranium-235 is about 8×10¹³ J. Chemical power and nuclear power are about six order of nuclear power.

Six energy anchors.

  • ** The early transformation of British industrialization**:t = 13.8 billion years (about 250 years ago). The United Kingdom experienced continuous changes in production, energy and transport from the second half of the eighteenth to the first half of the nineteenth century. [4]
  • ** Steam engine: extraction from mine to industrial power**: 1712 A.D. t ≈ 13.8 billion A.D. The steam engine converts thermal energy to mechanical work; the Nukeman utility steam engine in 1712 was a common reference, and Watt joined the separation condenser improvement later in 1760 at the end; it is not a single invention. [5]
  • ** Space Age: Sputnik 1**: 1957 t ≈ 13.8 billion A.D. On 4 October 1957, the Soviet Union launched the Spotnik 1, the first artificial Earth satellite in orbit; it is usually considered as the beginning of the space age, but the “era” is a later recapitulation. [6]
  • ** Carbon: Second 2p Electron added to 6 Proton, where the three α process **: Z = 6 [He]2s22p2. From boron to carbon, 2p from 1 Electron to 2; 11.261 eV ionizing energy, 67 pm radius and 2.55 electronegativity, allowing carbon to be constructed with four valence Electrons. [7]
  • ** Uranium: 92 Proton filled in 5f, 5f/6d staggered with no stability isotope**: Z = 92 [Rn] 5f36d17s2. The 5f of thirium added an Electron and the uranium became 5f3 6d1 7s2; and 5f staggered with 6d to produce multiple oxidizing states, which did not stabilize isotope, with 238U half-life at 4.468. [8]
  • ** Hydrogen fuel cells: operating temperature**: 353.15 K (example 80 °C). Proton exchange membrane fuel cells are often operating at about 60-80 °C; 80 °C is an example of a non-uniform temperature of all reactors, which is controlled by membrane materials, cooling and load. [9]

Connect the anchor to a line.

Six anchors break energy history into three lines: conversion technology (vapour engines, fuel cells), resource carriers (carbon and uranium) and social time (industrial revolution, space age). [4][5][6][7][8][9]

Energy conversion is not a replacement, but a superimposed one. Coal did not make wood disappear, oil did not make coal disappear, nuclear and renewable energy did not make Fossil fuel retreat immediately; each layer left infrastructure, benefits and path dependency. [4][5][6][7][8][9] Energy density determines transport and storage difficulties, and EROI determines net exergy volumes and grids and intermittentity determines how far renewables can go.
