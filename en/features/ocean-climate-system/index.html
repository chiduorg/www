The ocean-climate system: how Earth’s largest heat reservoir works

Machine-assisted draft · native review pending

The oceans absorb most of the additional heats of global warming and regulate climate through circulation, sea ice and sea gas exchange. The ocean is understood to understand heat waves, sea level and monsoons.

Thermal.

The water is larger than the water capacity, and the upper ocean mixed layer and deep circulation store huge amounts of the heat. The extra heat of global warming over the last few decades, about 90 percent of it goes into the ocean. [1] This means that sea tables are only a fraction of the ocean heat content.

Circulation

Wind, temperature and salinity drive ocean circulation. The Gulf of Mexico and the black tide are west-bundary streams, which transmit the heat to high latitudes; the Atlantic Ocean, which sends warm water northwards and sinks in cold water. [2] Circulation is not a static river, which bends, falls off the vortex and changes with climate.

I don't know.

Ocean heat waves are unusual events in which sea surfaces are consistently above seasonal benchmarks, affecting corals, fisheries and seagrass. Sea level rises are caused by thermal expansion, loss of glaciers and ice caps, and changes in land water. [3] These events are linked to atmospheric heat waves and precipitation extremes.

Frozen circle connections

Sea ice, ice sheets and years of frozen soil connect the oceans, atmosphere and land. Melting sea ice changes albedo and fresh water input; ice cap loss affects sea level; melting of frozen soil releases carbon and changes in surface hydrology. [1][3] These processes are lagging, and today's mission will affect the coming centuries.

Common misunderstandings

The common misconception is that “the ocean is so big that it doesn't matter how much hot water it pours”. Ocean heat capacity is large, but heat ingestion leads to swelling, acidification, oxygen deficiency and ecology pressure; it is not an unlimited buffer. Another misconception is that “sea level rises are even”; local depositions, ocean currents and gravitational changes cause regional differences.

How do we measure ocean heat?

Temperature and salinity profiles of the upper ocean of Argo buoy arrays provide longer-term records and satellite altitude and gravity data support. [1] Ocean thermal content is a volume fraction and is not the average of sea surface temperature; the treatment of deep and marginal oceans varies from one data set to another.

AMOC with climate

The Atlantic Ocean rotates the flow of warm water northwards and cold water sunk in the North Atlantic. Freshwater input, sea ice and windfield changes may weaken it; the IPCC assessment suggests that a complete collapse is unlikely in the twenty-first century, but there are significant uncertainties. [2] The film "The Ice Age Tomorrow" is not a scientific consensus.

Acidification and oxygen deficiency

Marine absorbers decline pH after CO2 and are affected by calcium carbonate shells and corals; nutrient salt input causes oxygen deficiency “dead zones” offshore. [3] These changes overlap with warming, changing the ecology system and fisheries.

Thermal waves and sea level

Ocean heat waves affect corals, seagrass and fish by continuing abnormally warm waters; sea level rise is caused by heat expansion, loss of glaciers and ice sheets, and changes in land water. [1][2] Both are related to increases in ocean heat, but timescale differs from the impact path.

Deep sea and ocean snow

Ocean snow is the organic particles that sink from the upper layer, bringing carbon to the deep sea; deep ocean circulation transports the heat, carbon and oxygen to the world. [1] Deep sea observation is thinner than the surface, and model is less bound to deep changes.

Corals and fisheries

Coral bleaching is triggered by continuous high temperatures, with the loss of symbiotic algae and the starvation of corals; the distribution of fish flows to the polar regions with rising temperatures. [2] Communities that depend on coral reefs and rising fisheries are the first to be hit.

Other Organiser

Argo, covering the upper layer, 2,000 metres, still lacks data for the deep sea, the outer sea and the polar regions; satellites can only look at the surface. [3] Improved ocean prediction depends on more profile, longer records and better coupling with model.

If there's only one thing to remember,

The ocean is thermal reservoir and buffer of the climate system: it absorbs most of the additional heat and redistributes the heat, carbon and nutrients.

Put the numbers back in scale.

The average depth of the oceans is about 3.7 kilometres; a significant proportion of the Earth ' s land will still be submerged. The ocean's volume is climate habitual source.

Connect the anchor to a line.

By placing these six anchors together, three layers of structure can be seen: the basin scale decides the heat and water vapour boomary conditions; the circulation and mixed layers redistribute the heat at latitudes and depths; and the sea surface temperature and ocean heat waves fall directly to corals, fisheries and coastal communities. [4][5][6][7][8][9]

These figures are not global averages at the same time. The width of the Pacific Ocean is the ocean basin scale, the current width of the Gulf is the flow scale, the thickness of the mixed layer varies according to season and latitude; the sea surface is the regional average, the ocean heat wave is used as the extreme threshold of the seasonal base, and the years of frozen tamperature is the annual average at a certain depth below ground. [4][5][6][7][8][9], so they cannot add or subtract directly, but can answer different scale questions.

For policy, this means that risk cannot be judged by sea watch temperature alone. Thermal content, sea level, acidization, oxygen deficiency and ecology are lagging behind, and the effect of emission reductions will not immediately appear on each indicator; monitoring must cover the whole chain, not just one average. [4][5][6][7][8][9]
