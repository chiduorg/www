Elements and life: the CHNOPS ledger

Machine-assisted draft · native review pending

Carbon, hydrogen, nitrogen, oxygen, phosphorus and sulphur form the main skeletons of life, but life is not the most abundant eliment. Enrichment, solubility, key capacity and energy metabolism determine which element can be used.

Six characters.

CHNOPS is the abbreviation of carbon, hydrogen, nitrogen, oxygen, phosphorus and sulphur. They form the bulk of atoms for proteins, nucleic acids, sugars, lipids and energy carriers; water provides solvents, carbon provides skeletons, phosphorus provides energy transfer and nucleic acid backbone, and sulphur is involved in certain proteins and electron transfers. [1 [2][4]

These six elementes are not the largest of the UNiverse. Hydrogen and helium are the most abundant, but helium is not involved in chemistry; oxygen and carbon are rich and can form stable covalent keys; nitrogen, phosphorus and sulphur are less abundant and cannot be replaced in key molecules. [1][3]

Why carbon?

Carbon can form four covalent keys that can stabilize with itself, hydrogen, oxygen, nitrogen and sulphur. The length and branch of the carbon chain is almost unlimited, providing a huge structural space for life. [1][7]

Carbon chemistry is not the “universe's only option”, but under known conditions it is an element that can bring complexity and stability together. Silicon can also be chained, but the Silicon-Oxygen key is easily hydrolytic in the water environment and the long chain is unstable. [1]

Water and oxygen.

The water is a polar solvent that dissolves ion and polar molecules and can also react through hydrogen keys. Its high-heat capacity makes it difficult for the cell to be punched through by tymperature, and its high-heating heat makes evaporation effective. [2][5]

Oxygen is a powerful oxidizer that provides a high energy density for aerobic breathing, but also creates a free radical. Life is controlled by antioxidation systems and insulation; early life evolves in an oxygen-depleting environment and later learns to use oxygen. [4] [5]

Rare phosphorus and sulphur

While phosphorus is used in organisms for nucleotide skeletons, ATPs and phosphorus resins, phosphorus in the crust is mainly present in phosphate minerals, with limited solubility. Phosphorus is often a limiting factor for freshwater and oceanic ecology. [1]

Sulphur can form a disulphur key to stabilize the protein structure; it can also be involved in electron transmission in iron sulfur clusters. It is particularly active in volcanic, hydrothermal and oxygen-deficient environments and may be the key to the early metabolism. [1][4]

Micromeasurement

Life also requires metals such as iron, zinc, copper, manganese and magnesium. Iron moves oxygen in blood proteins, magnesium catches light in chlorophyll and zinc acts in hundreds of enzymes. They are low in content but cannot be entirely replaced. [1][6]

Microlevel `micro' is relative to biological mass, not to the environment. Some metals are abundant in the environment but cannot be used in large amounts of life because of low solubility or high toxicity. [1][6]

Enrichment is not equal to availability

The high abundance of universe element does not necessarily exist on the planet ' s surface in a soluble and reactive form. Life requires a “feasible reservoir”, not a “total stock”. The scarcity of phosphorus, iron oxidation and nitrogen fixation are all problems of availability. [1][3]

This is also the reason why astrobiology is commonly used to discuss “elementaryness”: there is not enough water and there are carbon, nitrogen, phosphorus, sulphur and trace metals that can be used biologically. [3]

Method for reading CHNOPS

First, look at the role of element in molecules: skeleton, solvent, energy, catalytic. Second, look at its environmental chemistry: solubility, oxidation, mineral form. Third, see if it's a limiting factor. [1][4]

The separation of the three floors will make it possible to understand why only a few elements are used, but not randomly. [2][6]

From element to molecule

Element is just raw material, life is using molecules. Carbon skeletons determine structure, hydrogen and oxygen determine polarity and reaction, nitrogen appears in ammonia and alkali, phosphorus connects nucleic acid to energy carriers, and sulphur stabilizes protein and electron transfers. [1 [2][4]

The same proportion of element can have completely different functions in different molecules. Treating “element abundance” directly as a “life requirement” would ignore the chemical environment and molecular structure. [1][6]

Element ratio of life

The bulk of human dw is carbon, oxygen, hydrogen, nitrogen; phosphorus, sulphur are small but irreplaceable; potassium, sodium, calcium, magnesium, iron, zinc, etc. are involved in the form of ion or auxiliary factors. The ratio is not a reproduction of universe abundance, but a biochemical screening. [1 [2][6]

This is why, in the search for life outside the ground, it is important not only to look at whether there is water, but also whether carbon, nitrogen, phosphorus, sulphur and energy gradients are available. [3]

Microscale toxicity of element

Micromeasurement has dose windows. Iron, copper, zinc are necessary and excessive quantities produce free radicals or jamming enzymes. Life is maintained in concentrations with trans-shipment proteins, sequesterants and insulation, rather than simply “as much as possible”. [1][6]

This also explains why “supplementation calculus” cannot be separated from dose and form. The absorption and toxicity of different compounds of the same element can be significantly different. [1][6]

Dose and form are the last threshold for element in relation to life. [1][6]
