Absolute zero and negative temperature: 0 K is not 'no heat'

Machine-assisted draft · native review pending

K is the minimum temperature allowed by thermodynamics, not the particle is completely static. Negative temperature is not “cooler than 0 K”, but rather a more hot state in a given system than the inexhaustible temperature.

0 K not "no heat"

Absolute Zero is 0 K, about−273.15 °C. Based on classic images, temperature corresponds to the mean kinetic energy of the molecule; at 0 K, the heat movement seems to have stopped completely. [1][4]

Quantum mechanics does not allow this. Even at the lowest level of energy, particles have zero-point energy; their position cannot be zero at the same time as the kinetic. Helium liquids maintain quantum fluid behaviour at very low temperatures, and superfluid helium flows without viscosity, as evidenced by the interaction of zero points with condensation. [4] [5]

So, in the words of 0 K, more likely:** the system is at a minimum probability that energy can no longer be removed by cooling. ** Not “Nothing”, but “no more heat”.

Why can't we reach 0 K?

The third law of thermodynamics states that limited steps cannot cool the system to 0 K. Every step down, the heat is getting harder and harder; when approaching 0 K, heat capacity is nearing zero, and any small heat leaks carry the temperature back. [1][2]

The laboratory can do millikelvin or even lower equivalents, but reaching "0 K" is not a difficult task, but a maximum of termodynamics. We can only approach infinity and not arrive. [2] [3]

How does the thin chiller work?

Diluting chillers use helium-3 to mix with helium-4-separated: at very low temperatures, helium-3 takes heat from the dilution phase into the concentration phase and cools the surrounding environment. Multi-grade pre-cold allows the mixing room to reach millikelvinorder of magnitude. [3]

It is not “smuggles the heat to negative temperature”, but moves the heat by balancing it with entropy. PLTS-2000 provides calibration in a range of 0.9 mK to 1 K, indicating that the temperature area already has a replicable measurement system. [3]

What's a negative temperature?

Negative temperature is not "cooler than absolute zero." In the termodynamics, temperature's definition is associated with entropy's guide to energy; for the conventional system, temperature is positive. For some isolated systems with energy caps (e.g. nuclear cycling system, partial optical crystal system), the reversal of particle numbers can make the conductor negative. [5]

The negative testerature system is actually ** hotter than any positive testerature **: heat will flow from negative testerature to positive. It's not "cool to negative," it's "energy distribution backwards." [5]

Boer-Einstein condensed.

When the bovine is cooled below the threshold, a large number of particles occupy the same minimum energy, forming a bovine-Einstein condensation. It is not “atom stop motion”, but the wave function is the same at macroscale. [5]

BEC ' s observation requires very low temperature and highly manageable traps; it transforms "temperature" from the average kinetic energy of a single particle to the statistical nature of the whole quantum state. And that's why physics in the vicinity of 0 K can't be pushed by everyday intuition. [5]

The way to read the temperature scale

First, look at units: K, mK, nK not the same scale. Second, see the system: electron temperature, spintime, crystaltemperature can be different, and a "negativetemperature" label must indicate which degree of freedom. Thirdly, look at defination: negative témperature is established only in specific statistical systems and is not a common natural phenomenon. [2][5]

If you separate them, you can understand why "0 K's impossible" and "negative temperature exists" is not in conflict: one is the lower limit of thermodynamics and one is the energy distribution of a given system. Neither is synonymous with “colder”. [1][5][6]

What else is underneath the millelvin?

In addition to diluting chillers, there are laser cooling, evaporation cooling, heat insulation and nuclear cycling cooling. Each approach corresponds to different degrees of freedom: atom movement, Electron spin, nuclear spin. The so-called “minimum temperature” often refers only to a certain degree of freedom and not to all the energy of the whole material. [2] [3]

For example, the atom gas flat-temperature can be lower to Nakelvin, but the internal Electron state may still be in a stimulating state; the typature can be lower and the crystaltemperature is higher. When reading the experimental paper, it is necessary to see which part of the test is measured by the test. [2] [3]

This is also the reason why the “negative test” is prone to misunderstanding: it usually refers to the equivalent of the rotation or the distribution of the number of particles, not the testerature reading of the whole sample. Treating the equivalent as daily temperature leads to the erroneous conclusion that “better than absolute zero”. [5] [6]

Temperature is not a ruler.

Temperature measures a macrobalance, not the speed of a single particle. Infrared thermometry is based on surface radiation, thermal electrons are based on contact point temperature, and the gas temperature is based on average molecular kinetic energy; the three can give different numbers to the same object. [1][2]

At very low temperatures, thermal exposure is weaker, the heat capacity is smaller, and the tamperature meter itself is likely to disturb the sample. The so-called “100 mK” requires a description of the temperature measurement method, the caliberization chain and thermal anchor, rather than a single count. [2] [3]
