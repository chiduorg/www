Human time perception: why a minute can feel long

Machine-assisted draft · native review pending

The clocktime is all the same, while subjectivity changes with attention, memory, age and context. It cannot be “read out a number” directly, but can only be estimated by comparison with the mandate.

Two times.

The clocktime is a periodic motion definition, the second is the SI base unit; it is even, re-emergible and does not change as people feel. Subjectivetime is an estimate of spacing, influenced by attention, emotion, memory and physical state; the two are not interchangeable. [2][6] Again, for a minute, the wait may be long and the focus may be short. This is not the result of the word “suspicion”, but rather of the weighting of event density, change and expectations by the brain. [6]

Why can't a minute be long?

When the environment is monotonous and no new information is available, the brain frequently checks “how long it has passed”, and each point makes the interval longer; when the task is occupied and the event is intense, the number of inspections is reduced and thetime is “compressed”. [6] The memory is also involved. When looking back at a paragraph, we would feel that if there were many retrievable events in it, and if it was repeated every day without a mark, it would feel “nothing left”. [6]

Age to Proportion

Children often feel that one year is long and adults often feel that one year is short. One explanation is the proportional effect: one year at the age of 10 is one tenth of life, and at the age of 40 it is only one tenth; the other is less fresh experience and less memory marks. [6] Proportional effects are not strict laws and different experiments give different intensity. It reminds us that subjective time does not have a unified conversion and cannot be used as a physical conclusion to “the older the time”. [2][6]

Attention, emotion and body.

Anxiety causes excessive surveillance of the time, and pain increases the distance between the wait and pain; contrary to the flow of heart, the target of the mission is captured and the time sense is reduced. Emotion is not an add-on, it directly changes the strategy of the job that Time estimated. [6] Day and night rhythms and body temperature and vigilance also affect Time feeling. Flight across time zones, lack of sleep and shifts will cause both “the moment” and “how long” to fail; this is not a broken clock, but a temporary mismatch between the body clock and the outside clock. [2][6]

How?

Psychologists measure the subjective length of time using producer tasks, reproduction missions, oral estimates and intervals; neuroscientific reactions, eye movements, brain electrons and fMRI search-related activities. They measure the estimated behaviour, not “subjectivetime itself”. [6] All the conditions for measurement are: type of mission, age, sleep, emotions and experience the day before. It makes no sense to leave with an "average overestimation of 20%" only. [2][6]

I'm sorry.

Subjective time is not relativity about time expansion. At daily speeds, the movement brings with it a time that is so small that it is impossible to distinguish between people's feelings; it's two sets of scale that make it a relativism to say "happy times pass ". [2][6] This page can say that clocktime is measurable and subjectivity is comparable but not directly readable; both are real and answer different questions. [2][6]

Deep reading: experimental paradigm

The forward estimate requires the testee to estimate “the duration of the mission”, and the retrospective estimate requires an ex post facto judgement as to “the length of the previous paragraph”; the two often give different results. The experiment also manipulates mission difficulty, wait, feedback and emotion to separate attention from memory. [2][6]

In-depth reading: memory and expectations

When one expects something to end soon, the watch is often looked at and the time is shredded; when one is immersed in continuous tasks, fewer checkpoints and the time sense is compressed. It is not expected to be the only factor, but it can explain why the same clocktime is “density” different in different situations. [6]

Deep reading: individual and cultural differences

Age, sleep, anxiety, depression, attention deficit and cross-cultural time view can affect estimates. On average, groups can describe trends and cannot be directly integrated into one person; clinical evaluations require standardized tasks and models, rather than a sentence that “I thinktime is getting faster”. [2][6]

In-depth reading: actionable conclusions

It is usually more effective to increase the number of engagements and to reduce the frequency of the watch if a waiting period is to be “short”, than to stare at the seconds; to add fresh experience and retrievable markers if it is to be “longer” in memory. The two changes are subjective, not clocktime. [2][6]

Continue: Time estimated tasks

Common tasks include oral estimates, key replication, producer assignments and time. Different missions do not measure the same capability and the result cannot be directly averaged as a “time sense”. [2][6]

Continue: Sleep and fatigue

Inadequate sleep reduces vigilance and alters the time estimate; flying across time zones puts the body clock at a different position than the outside. When judging “time change”, sleep and medicine are excluded. [2][6]

Continue: pain and waiting

Pain and waiting often magnifies the time sense, as attention goes back to “not over”. Fragmentation and giving progress feedback can change the length of the subjective wait, but not the actual length. [6]

Continuation: age effect

The relationship between age and time estimates is not consistent in different studies; proportional effects, life tempo, fresh experience and experimental design may affect results. Do not take personal experience as a universal law. [2][6]

Continue: Recording mode

Long-term observations record dates, sleep, tasks, emotions and estimates; individual times are not enough to support conclusions, and trends depend on days and circumstances. [2][6]

Something to look back on.

Clocktime and subjectivity are two sets of problems: the former is defined by periodic motion, while the latter is weighted by attention, memory, emotional and physical state. Experiments can measure behaviour, but cannot read “subjectivetime itself”. Proportional effects, fresh experience and sleep affect outcomes, and groups on average cannot substitute for individual assessments. They want to wait for “shorter”, to increase the number of missions available, to reduce the frequency of reading, to “longer” memories and to add fresh experience and searchable marks. They change feelings, not physics. [2][6]
