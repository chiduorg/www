Measurement, error and significant figures: how much can a number be trusted?

Machine-assisted draft · native review pending

Measurement does not read a number, but gives a number, unit and uncertainty. Accuracy and precision, random error and systematic error, significant figures and decimal places answered different questions; writing the results of the medical process into precise values without error amounts to concealing its most critical baseary.

Three sets of a number

A available measurement result must have at least three parts: values, units, and uncertainty. It is not enough to write only “24 cm” because the reader does not know whether this is a calibration to millimetres, or a full value from the perimeter of the rule; it is not enough to write only “9.109×10⁻³¹” because, in the absence of units, the value has no physical meaning. [1][2]

Uncertancy is not a “possibly wrong” guess, but a quantitative description of the degree of fragmentation of the results. It tells us where the true value falls in the given level of confidence and under the conditions of measurement. No figures are available for ranking, but it is not appropriate to judge whether the two outcomes are really different. [1][3]

Accuracy is not synonymous with precision

Accuracy (accuracy) refers to the degree to which the results of the meeting are close to the true value; precidention (prescription) refers to the degree to which the repetition of the meeting is close to each other. A ruler that is always one centimetre to the left can be very precisionary, but not anaccuracy; a set of far-off readings may be close to the real value on average, but not precision. [1][6]

These two properties correspond to different error structures. The system turns all readings in the same direction, and random error lets readings spread around the average. Calibration can reduce the systemeror, and adding repetition can estimate random error, but the two cannot be replaced. [1][3]

Where's Error from?

More than "misreading the scale." Instrument resolution, environmental temperature, measurementdefinition, operator readings, uneven samples, model assumptions, all enter the results. In symmetrical terms, air floats change readings; in radar, asteroid mass is estimated, shape model and density assumptions change results. [1][3]

Thus, the conditions are usually to be written in the report: at what levelerature, with what instrument, by what means, how many times. By deleting the conditions and leaving only one number, the reader cannot judge whether it can be compared to another result. [2]

Significant figures are not decorations.

Significant figures indicates the number of places in the value, not " writing more and more precisely ". 24.0 centimetres at millimeter scale and 0 at the end are reading places; writing 24.000 centimetres implies precision below millimetres, which the instrument does not support. [1]

There is also a commandary rule: intermediate calculations should not be rounded repeatedly, with the final result cut by the most uncertain input; multiplied by the relative uncertainty, plus the absolute uncertainty. The scientific count is clearer than a string of zeros for a large physical mass of mass. [1][2]

How do you read it?

Uncertancy has two types of A and B source. Category A is estimated to be statistically dispersed by repetition; category B is estimated by non-statistical information such as instrument certificates, resolution, known deviations, etc. The two categories are synthesized and synthesized into a synthetic standard uncertainty, which is then extended to the confidence interval by the covering factor. [3]

When reading a result, look at whether it is standard or extended and at the coverage factor. It is common to misread “±0.1” as absolute or to add two uncertainty directly. [3]

Two cases: metrics and objectsmass

The length of the finger in a normal, straight-foot scale with a resolution of 1 mm, and the readings also add value, and the uncertainty of the single meeting may be 1-2 mm. If the finger is about 7 centimetres long, the officer is about 2%; if it's a 7 mm small object, the same absolute error becomes more than 20%. The relative impact of Error depends on the size of the measurement itself. [5]

Asteroids are harder. The mass in Tutati is 5.0×10¹³ kg, only two significant figures, and the Comet in Khare is 2.2×10¹⁴ kg, with the same two remaining. This is not a “slugger”, but rather a ceiling of precision determined jointly by radar dispersion, shape model and density assumptions. A few more words only create a false sense of precision. [4]

Units and Traceability

Units are not labels, but agreements that can be made. The metre is defined by the distance light travels in a vacuum in a given time, seconds by caesium leaps to frequency, kg by Plank constant after 2019. To be comparable elsewhere, a measurement result must go back to these benchmarks along the chain. [2][5]

Traceability is not “absolutely correct”. Each calibration introduces uncertainty, and the longer the chain, the larger the end uncertainty. The value of the calibration certificate is to show you how much each part of the chain contributes, not to give you an infinity and accuracy. [2] [3]

Common misunderstandings

The first misperception is that “the instrument shows a few, just a few”. Numerical screens can give many bits, but their effectiveness is limited by sensors, noise and caliber. The second misconception is that “error is the maximum impairment”. Extremely poor is just a rough, scattered description, not a complete uncertainty. [1][3]

The third misunderstanding is that “error is written as ±0 as anaccuracy”. ±0 usually means that there is no assessment of uncertinty, rather than no error. The fourth misconception is that “significant figures are more scientific”. In cross-scale comparisons, the retention of excessive digits would instead mask the difference between order of magnitude. [1][6]

An actionable checklist

If you see any measurement numbers, you can ask in order: What units? What's that? How much is uncertainty, what kind? Under what conditions? Does a signature figure match uncertainty? Does it compare with another number on the same medical condition condition? These six questions are more useful than remembering a rule of significant figures. [1 [2][3]

Measurement is a knowledge of boundary. A good number does not pretend to be infinity; rather, it writes about its baseary; the reader therefore knows how much it supports. It's part of the scientific expression. [1][3]
