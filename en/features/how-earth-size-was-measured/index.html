How big is Earth? From a stick to satellite geodesy

Machine-assisted draft · native review pending

In the third century B.C., Elatossini estimated the Earth's perimeter with a shadow distance from city; today, satellite lasers, VLBI and gravity field model measure the Earth's shape to centimetres. This is a history of measration from geometry to time and space.

Geometry of a stick.

Elatossini measured the angles of the sun and lead plume from summer to midday in Alexandria, approximately 7.2°, approximately 1/50 around the circle. He took the distance between Alexander and Sainny, multiplied by 50, and received the Earth's perimeter. [1] This reasoning assumes only that solar light is near parallel, two places on the same midday line and is known at a distance; it does not require more details about the Earth than the sphere.

How accurate are the ancient results?

According to Egypt's status, the results of Elatossini differ by a few percentage points to a dozen percentage points from the perimeter of 40,000 km of magnitude today. [1][2] This is not “accurate knowledge of ancients”, but a geometrical model victory: both angles and distances carryerror, but the correct approach is not wrong.

From ball to ellipse.

In the eighteenth century, Earth was found not to be a perfect sphere: the equatorial radius was about 6,378 kilometres, the polar radius was about 6,357 kilometres, and the flat rate was about 1/298. Triangular networks, measurement and gravity observation transform the Earth's shape from a philosophical problem to a calculable ellipse. [2] Today's common use is WGS 84 and so on.

Satellite and cm Earth

Satellite laser ranging, VLBI, GNSS and gravity satellites (e.g. GRACE) measure Earth shape, gravity field and plate motion. They do not “look” directly at the Earth, but rather measure signal transmissiontime, phase and orbital disturbances. [3] The geoids are not ellipses, but gravitational parallels; local surfaces, mountains and subsurface masss allow them to rise and fall.

Why are you still checking?

The Earth's shape changes with times: plate movement, post-icing echoes, sea level rise, groundwater extraction and earthquarkes change gravity and elevation benchmarks. [3] Centimetre-scale measurements are not intended to pursue a permanent number, but rather to provide a common baseline for navigation, flood Model, glacial mass losses and sea level monitoring.

Common misunderstandings

The common misconception is that “the world was still thought to be flat by the time of Columbus”. Medieval European scholars generally know that the Earth is a sphere, and Columbus does not “prove” that the Earth is round. Another misunderstanding was to treat the results of Elatossini as modern precision; he gave the correct geometrical estimate of order of magnitude, not centimetre-scale measurement.

How does the angle and distance become the perimeter?

The key to Elatossini is not “know that the Earth is round”, but to turn two measurements into calculable geometry. Alexander is midday around 7.2°, equal to 1/50 of the circle; the perimeter is 50 times the distance as long as the distance between the two cities is known. [1] However, there are different versions of the length of stadion: approximately 39,375 kilometres per week if calculated at 157.5 metres; and approximately 46,620 kilometres at 185 metres. It's about 40,008 kilometres in length today, so his result is “order of magnitude right”, depending on the unit conversion.

Ellipse, geoid and GPS

The Earth rotates the equator, with an equatorial radius of about 6,378 km and a polar radius of about 6,357 km. Earthmeasurement approximates the shape of a given ellipse, and then describes the geometrically gravitational level; GPS coordinates are not “sea level”, but rather relative to a mathematical model. [2] Mountain ranges, trenches, ice caps and underground density allow the gravitational field to rise and fall, so the “Earth radius” is never a single number accurate to millimetres.

Why is the precision of measurement still improving?

Satellite laser range is measured from Earth station to satellite reflector to time to millimetre class; VLBI uses radio telescopes simultaneously observation-like bodies to measure baseline length; and GNSS uses satellite clock differentials and orbit to calculate position. [3] These technologies place Earth shape, plate motion and sea level change in the same dynamic benchmark. Measurement Earth is not a completed project, but a continuous renewal of the Reference Framework.

If Earth is perfect,

If the Earth is a perfect sphere, the equator should have exactly the same gravity as the polar level, with all long lines. Actual gravity is about 9.78 m/s2 in the equator and 9.83 m/s2 in the polar region, with variations from centrifugal strength and flat ratio. [2][3] This difference is sufficient to influence the clock, the satellite orbit and the Earth-measurement; it is also one of the early-tested windows of the Newton gravity theory.

Unit conversion pit

The ancient length units often follow urban states, times and goods. Stadion may refer to the length of the stadium runway, or to a specific unit in Egypt; the same number is changed to one conversion, with a perimeter difference of over 7,000 kilometres. [1] So reading the ancient measurement, ask first, “What size did he use” and then ask, “Is the result correct?”

Why do we have to measure the Earth today?

Modern Earthmeasurement supports satellite navigation, sea level monitoring, seismic shape change and glacial mass change. The plate moves a few centimetres per year and the ice sheet loss changes the gravity field, all of which require a reference framework of millimeters to centimetres. [2] The Earth is not a static sphere, but a continuous transformation system.

What can a classroom experiment measure?

Using a pole, a ruler and a known north-south distance, Elatossini's thinking can be repeated and the approximates of the Earth's perimeter measured. Error is from a video reading, whether the two cities share the same route, terrain and distance. [3] Its value is not precision, but the transformation of “Earth size” into a testable geometry.

A common reverse example.

"The Earth is round" does not mean "The Earth is perfect." Equator drumming, mountain mass, ice caps and sea level allow gravity to rise and fall; but “not a perfect ball” is not the same as “frozen shape”. Landmeasurement uses ellipses and geoids to define deviations in order to both recognize irregularities and maintain computing potential.

  2. **station's unit dispute why is it important? ** The same angle is multiplied by different distances and can vary by thousands of kilometres; the ancient measurement's “error” is often from units rather than geometry.
  3. ** What's the difference between geodesy and reference ellipse? ** Ellipse is a mathematical approximation and geoid is a gravitational equivalent; the former is easy to calculate and the latter is close to the real sea level.

If there's only one thing to remember,

The core of the Earth's size history of measurement is not “old-fashioned intelligence”, but rather the same geometry chain of angles, distance and units. Any chain changes units, the results change.

Put the numbers back in scale.

The Earth is condensed into a ball of 1 metre diameter, with an equator and a polar radius of about 3 mm, a Everest of about 0.7 mm and a deepest trench of about 0.9 mm. The Earth looks round, but the millimetre class deviation is enough to make satellite navigation subject to modification.

Connect the anchor to a line.

Five anchors transform the Earth from a spherical, mass-age planet: 12,742 kilometres in diameter, 8,848.86 metres in snow surface of Everest, 3,474.8 kilometres in diameter, age of the Earth approximately 4.54 billion years, mass5.972×10²⁴ kg. [4][5][6][7][8]

Elatosini's geometry only gives the ball a perimeter; today's Earthmeasurement deals with ellipse, geoids, tides and plate motion. The precision of measurement is not "corrected" from the old value, but a gradual return of the Earth from the ideal sphere to a real object with shape and mass distribution. [4][5][6][7][8]
