An Early ARPANET Connection

Machine-assisted draft · native review pending

On 29 October 1969, an early ARPANET host signal was transmitted between UCLA and Stanford Research. This is an important node in the development of the Internet; cross-network agreements and their dissemination are subsequent ongoing work.

What is it?

This article records the early mainframe connection of the ARPANET network, not the date of the birth of the “Internet” as a whole. ARPANET is an experimental group exchange network funded by the United States Department of Defense ' s Advanced Research and Planning Agency (ARPA) to connect decentralized research institutions ' computers. The break-up of the message into small packages by group exchanges and its reorganization at the end after transmission by different routes is different from the exchange of circuits for traditional line telephones. [1][2]

How?

Britannica documented that in late 1969 the UCLA sent the first group exchange to the Stanford Institute (SRI); the Internet Society's review gave a more detailed process: in September 1969, BBN installed the first interface message processor in UCLA, and a month later the SRI accessed and sent the first host to the mainframe, with four nodes at the end of the year. [1][2] The initial login was not smooth: the system crashed when entering the first two letters of “LOGIN” and then successfully retryed. These are works records and participant reviews, not single-date monuments.

Time Coordinates and Measurement condition

This station uses 29 October 1969 as a navigation node, a common date for UCLA-SRI first-time host communication; the upper seconds of the axis are sorted by the approximate year. The TCP papers of 1969, 1974 and the TCP/IP of 1983, which completely replaced NCP, are different levels of road signs; the former are single connections and the latter are cross-network agreements. [1][2]

Common misunderstandings

A common misconception is that “the Internet was born in 1969 and is available to the public”. At that time, there were only a few research nodes, electron mail became a popular application only in 1972, and the World Wide Web was introduced only in 1989. [1][2] There is also a misunderstanding that it is a purely military project; the ARPA motive includes military communications concerns, but its actual use is dominated by the sharing of academic resources.

Relationship to other phases

ARPANET is the technology precursor to the Internet, and its cluster exchanges later supported the World Wide Web, mobile communications and cloud computing with TCP/IP. [1][2] This change has occurred over decades compared to the industrial and scientific revolutions, but its institutional basis remains the long-term funding of universities, the military and the Government.
