Columbus's First Transatlantic Voyage

Machine-assisted draft · native review pending

In 1492, Columbus crossed the Atlantic Ocean to the Caribbean, opening up continuous contacts between Europe and the Americas; in 534 it was slightly this year, and the term “discovery” was controversial.

What is it?

In 1492, Columbus, supported by the Spanish Crown, led a flotilla to the west, arriving at Guanahani in the Bahamas (now San Salvador) and then entering the Caribbean Sea. His voyage transformed Europe ' s ties with the Americas from an occasional contact to a continuous process and opened the chain of colonial conquest, population transfer and disease transmission. [1][2]

How?

The evidence included a fleet log, a letter from Columbus to the Crown, a report published in 1493, a nautical chart and a post-mortem file. Britannica documented his life and four voyages; the exhibit at the Library of the United States Congress showed his reports to Ferdinand and Isabella after the 1492 voyage, as well as his encounter with the Tanoes. [1][2] Texts, printed copies and archaeological materials are mutually authenticated.

Time Coordinates and Measurement condition

Entry takes 534 years from today for navigation, corresponding to 1492. The three nodes of “Arrival to the Caribbean Islands” “Return Report Releases” of “Arrival from the Port of Palos” were divided between 1492 and 1493. [1][2] The number from this year is an integer conversion made in 2026, with the upper seconds of the axis used only for sorting. And 1492 is not the starting point for the history of the Native Americans, but is only a point of contact for Europe.

Common misunderstandings

The common misconception is to write it as “discover a new continent” as if the Americas were uninhabited. There are complex societies in the Caribbean and the Americas, and groups such as the Tenos have their own political, religious and agricultural systems. [1][2] Another misunderstanding is that navigation is understood as a single act of hero; it relies on royal contracts, shipbuilding, navigational experience and multiple crews, with consequences such as conquest and disease.

Relationship to other phases

The voyage was at the beginning of the era of great navigation, with the accumulation of navigational technology from the Middle Ages to the revival of the arts, followed by the colonial empire, the slave trade and the global exchange of species. It is almost parallel to the spread of print, religious reform and the unprecedented speed with which information, population and pathogens move across the oceans. [1][2] On the time axis, 1492 marks the acceleration point of exposure, not the beginning of American history.
