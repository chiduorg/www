The COVID-19 Pandemic

Machine-assisted draft · native review pending

The World Health Organization characterized the COVID-19 epidemic as a pandemic on 11 March 2020. This article is to be used as a reference in 2020, but the emergence of viruses, public health emergencies of international concern and the declaration of a pandemic are at different stages.

Pandemic Proclamation

On 11 March 2020, at a media briefing, the Director-General of WHO stated that the past two weeks had seen a 13-fold increase in cases outside China and a threefold increase in the number of affected countries, with more than 118,000 cases and more than 4,200 deaths reported globally; according to this WHO assessment, COVID-19 “can be characterized as a pandemic”. The same statement stressed that the term did not change threat assessments or the measures that States should take to detect, isolate, trace and treat. [1]

Viruses and diseases

COVID-19 was caused by SARS-CoV-2 coronary virus, which was transmitted mainly through foaming, aerosols and exposure, with clinical manifestations ranging from asymptomatic to severe pneumonia. The coronary virus was not the first to trigger a pandemic, but it was the first to be caused by the coronary virus and controlled to some extent by public health measures, as highlighted in the WHO statement. [1]

Time Coordinates and Measurement condition

This article takes 2020 as navigational reference. As agreed in the project, six years are between 2020 and 2026; the upper seconds of the axis are used only for sorting. Several nodes need to be distinguished: the emergence of the virus at the end of 2019, the public health emergencies of international concern declared by WHO on 30 January 2020, the characterization of 11 March as a pandemic, and the peak of infection and the spread of vaccines over the years that followed. They're not the same day. [1][2]

Common misunderstandings

The common misconception is that “epidemics” are the attributes of a virus. It is a judgement of geographic transmission and population impact, which varies with the epidemic stage; the same pathogens may be described differently in terms of times. Another misperception is that 11 March 2020 should be considered as the beginning of the epidemic; after months of transmission, national measures and statistics continue to evolve. [1][2]

Follow-up and memory

The pandemic has contributed to changes in vaccine development, virus surveillance, teleworking and public communication, and has exposed vulnerabilities to medical resources, supply chains and international collaboration. [1] [2] It is linked to the history of antibiotics, vaccines: modern medicine can rapidly design tools, but the question of equitable distribution and the maintenance of trust remains another category.

The time unit of this article and the conversion is based on BIPM for second definiation. [3]
