Earliest Continental Crust

Machine-assisted draft · native review pending

The oldest known evidence of evolution of the continental crust came about 4 billion years ago from the Canadian akasta and its zircon. The point here is that minerals and rock records are not the “first continent” that finish the puzzle one day.

What is it?

The “first continent” refers to the oldest known and evolving crust of land, rather than the first whole continent on the Earth's surface. About 4 billion years ago, there were granite rock rich in silicon aluminium on Earth; this record was kept by the Acasta line of lava in north-western Canada. There, researchers found zircon, some 4.03 billion years old, as well as a cloud-blinking long linen piece of jatropha some 4.02 billion years ago. They show that the Earth was able to produce lighter, more complex crust at an early stage, rather than just ergogenic sea crust. [1][2]

How?

Zircon is a weather-resistant and recycled mineral, and uranium-leadiisotope systems are like a caliber clock. The researchers obtained crystallization age using the ratio of uranium, lead and its isotope in mass spectroscopy mass spectrometers; and the magma source and whether it was modified by surface water was determined by isotope. Rock in Acasta undergoes multi-temporal deformation and deformation, thus requiring a distinction between the age of original crystallization and the age of later transformation. Mineral particles can be very old, but the continent they leave is not necessarily formed at the same time. [1][2]

Why does this rule have this coordinates?

Navigation places coordinates around 4.03 billion years ago, corresponding to the usual round-intensity of the oldest zircon evidence. Conversion to the Time axis from Big Bang is about 9.77 billion years. This position means “the most known evidence of the most old crustal material” and not “the first continent's birthday”. If an older rock or zircon is found in the future, the coordinates may need to be moved forward and the values may be adjusted if evidence from a place is re-explained. [1]

Common misunderstandings

The common misconception is that an old zircon's age is directly considered “continental formation”. zircon can crystallize in magma or be removed as debris into younger sedimentary rocks; it proves the presence of ancient crust in the source area and does not necessarily prove that stable continental blocks existed at that time. Another misperception is that the earliest continent is a single, complete and rapidly integrated supercontinent. The early crust is more likely to be a small, scattered, repeatedly melted and reconstructed block. [2]
