Nuclear Timescale

Machine-assisted draft · native review pending

The strong interaction is characterized by a time of approximately 0QX seconds, which is the typical time frame for the completion of a nuclear process with a nuclear power guide. It describes continuity, not the absolute moment when a nuclear reaction occurs.

What is it?

The strong interaction is characterized by a time of approximately 10⁻²³ seconds, which is typical of the time during which the nuclear power-guided strong and nuclear processes can be completed. It describes “the duration of such processes” as not “when a nuclear reaction occurs”. [1] A common intuitive extrapolation is that the nuclear force range is about 1 fm, and the distance is only about 3×10⁻²⁴ seconds, so the strong process is limited to 10⁻²³ seconds order of magnitude. [2]

How?

The resonance in particle physics provides direct evidence: they are short-lived by powerful decay so that they do not leave a visible trail that can only be inferred from energy width. Britannica states that the resonance "there is only light through the times it needs" and that it collapses in 10⁻²³ seconds. [1] Strong action distance of about 10⁻¹⁵ metres, this scale combined with light speed gives the same order of magnitude. [2] The accelerator does not change the width of the mass spectra through the measurement to release this very short-lived conversion.

Value and Measurement condition

10⁻²³ seconds are order of magnitude, not the precise life of a single process; different resonances can be up to 10⁻²⁴ to 10⁻²⁰ seconds. [1] Weak interactions and electromagnetic interactions are much slower, corresponding to longer life periods, respectively, and therefore cannot be applied to “nuclear processes”. Citiation should indicate whether it is a strong decay, a nuclear exchange or a high-energy collision feature. [1][2]

How long does this last, not when?

This is the timescale of the process, not the absolute point of events. For individual nuclear reactions, quantum mechanics give only probability and time distribution, and 10⁻²³ seconds describe typical time continuity.

Common misunderstandings

One of the misunderstandings was to consider10⁻²³ seconds as “what happened at that moment”. It describes the fast process within the nuclear and the strong, and is not related to the early UNiverse absolutetime coordinates. [1] Another misconception is the mixing of the powerful time with the nuclear decayhalf-life; the half-life of many radioactivity nuclides has been dominated by the weak role for several years.

Relationship with other scale

10⁻²³ seconds are 10 million times shorter than the aom jump life (approximately 10⁻⁸ seconds) and about 10²³ times shorter than a heartbeat. It's one of the fastest features known to be in the physical process, which connects up to the microsecond heat of the quark-gel plasma, and down to the limit of Planktime. [1][2]
