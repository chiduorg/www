The Bronze Age

Machine-assisted draft · native review pending

The Bronze Age is a historical interval characterized by the use of copper, starting with the geologic age and changing with the region. Some parts of the Near East, Europe and Asia are often summarised from about 3,000 to 1,000 B.C., and cannot be used as global unity.

What is it?

The Bronze Age is a historical period characterized by the extensive use of copper, which usually refers to alloys of copper and tin. Bronze was first found in the copper quarries and used in the copper quarries, and the main late was the copper tin alloy; the name emphasized material, but social change was not determined by material alone. It goes up and up with the years, down to the iron age. The archaeological vocabulary of the French Ministry of Culture makes it clear that, in areas where classification applies, such as the Near East, Europe and Asia, the years of copper are roughly 3000 B.C. to 1,000 B.C., but there are wide variations across regions. [1]

How?

Evidence was drawn from copper objects, metallurgical residues, mineral sources and trading networks. The researchers used alloy components to judge the copper tin ratio, trace the metal source with lead isotope, and combine the statigraphy with the competitor to create the georgical age. Britannica states that Greece and China entered the copper age 3,000 B.C., and that Britain is about 1900 B.C., and much more so about 1,000 B.C. [1][2]

Common misunderstandings

There is a common misconception that the “blownness age” is considered a phase of worldwide synchronization, or that entering the brass age is tantamount to a state, city and text. In fact, copper technology can exist in different social structures; text appears earlier in the two river basins and is not the same as copper use. [1][2] Nor can the development phase of society as a whole be judged by a brass weapon.

Relationship to other phases

The pre-blown-blown agricultural villages, the post-blown iron age, were interwoven with the early words, the city and the distance trade. After copper, iron is becoming more widespread with larger raw materials and harder tools, but transformations also vary from region to region. [1][2]
