Origins of Cities

Machine-assisted draft · native review pending

This article uses Uruk of Mesopotamia as one of the earliest evidences of the city, taking 3,500 B.C. as a round-the-course. “city” depends on the size of the population, functional division and intimacy, and there is still a discussion as to whether early large-scale gatherings count as city.

What does "city" mean?

City isn't just a lot of houses. Archaeology usually depends on the size of the population, the division of occupations, public buildings, storage and redistribution, and the dependence of city on the surrounding agricultural hinterland. Only densely populated and lacking such relationships may still be classified as large villages. When you change, you change the list of the earliest city and the time. [1]

The earliest evidence of city.

Uruk in southern Mesopotamia is often one of the earliest city. Khan Academy's readings consider the periods between 3600 and 2600 B.C. to be the zones in which its city features were formed: social hierarchies, specialized occupations, scripts, religions and memorial buildings were concentrated during that period, reaching tens of thousands of people. Britannica, for its part, shows that the Sumerian region has gathered between 4,500 and 4,000 B.C., and that the Sumerians arrived in about 3,300 B.C. and have formed several urban states by the 3000 B.C. [1][2]

The earlier large gatherings such as Chatahouyuk in Anatolia are often compared. Their houses are sprawled and densely populated, but there is a lack of paperwork, memorial public buildings, like Uruk, and visible integration; hence the term “city” depends on the type of definition used. [1]

Common misunderstandings

The common misconception is that “when the first city emerges, city goes from one place to the global diffusion”. In fact, Mesopotamia, the Nile Valley, the Indus River Basin and the northern part of China form separate centres of city in different times, in different shapes and systems. Another misperception is that text is a sine qua non for city; some city does not leave words that can still be identified through building, storage and demographic evidence. [1][2]

City and Text, Country

City makes large sections of the population dependent on warehousing, taxation and division of labour, and the text is initially entangled with accounts and administration. City has also stimulated currency, law and memorial buildings and increased competition for surrounding land, water and labour. [1][2] From agricultural village to city, it is not simply a process of scaling up, but of reorganizing social relations.
