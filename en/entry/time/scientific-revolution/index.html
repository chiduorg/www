Milestones of Early Modern Science

Machine-assisted draft · native review pending

Recent European natural studies have evolved into a community of experiment, communication, discussion and publication. The Mathematics of Natural Philosophy of 1687 is one of the identifiable nodes of the literature, not the date of the simultaneous birth of science throughout the world.

What is it?

The “scientific revolution” usually refers to a recent shift in the way European natural research is conducted: observation, experimentation, communication, debate and publication have evolved into a verifiable knowledge community. It is not a single discovery by a discipline or a genius, but a continuous institutional and methodological change that took place between the 15th and 17th centuries. Britannica summarized its time range from about 1,400 to 1690 and linked it to the work of Copernicus, Kepler, Galileo, Newton and others. [2]

How?

Historic materials include the Society ' s archives, correspondence, periodicals, instruments and works. According to the archives of the Royal Society, its first meeting was held on 28 November 1660, at an early stage to collect information on natural philosophy through correspondence and to organize experiments, discussions and publications. [1] These records show how knowledge is openly shared and tested, and not just a certain law is presented. [1][2]

Time Coordinates and Measurement condition

It uses the 1687 Mathematics of Natural Philosophy as a navigation node; this is a recognizable documentary event and is not the starting point for “recent science”. Between 1687 and 2026 339 years; the upper seconds of the axis are used only for sorting. Publications, theoretical acceptance and institution-building each have their own timescale. [1][2]

Common misunderstandings

A common misconception is that the scientific revolution will be seen as a day when the whole of Europe will be “a different set of methods” or that Newton's writings will be conceived as a complete scientific method. In fact, the day-to-day functioning of learning societies, communication and periodicals, as well as earlier knowledge traditions with other regions, are involved in this transformation. [1][2] Nor can the “science revolution” be equated with the industrial revolution; the latter is a transformation of production and energy organizations.

Relationship to other phases

The scientific revolution, which provided the knowledge and instrumentation base for industrialization in the eighteenth to nineteenth centuries, was not the only reason; the industrial revolution, in turn, changed the funding and instrumentation conditions for scientific research. [1][2] Scientific research in the 17th century was much smaller than in the Great Science of the 20th century and today ' s space telescope, but public communication and repertoireable norms have begun to take shape.
