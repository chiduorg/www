Early Evidence of the Genus Homo

Machine-assisted draft · native review pending

Ledi-Geraru, Ethiopia, is a low-level fossil that provides evidence of membership between 2.8 million and 2.75 million years ago. Unlike the history of the intelligent species to which we belong, the history of humans cannot be confused with the origin of the two.

What is it?

The early Fossil evidence in this article, which deals with the personal status of the person (Homo), is not equivalent to “human” as a whole, much less intellectual. The ledi-Geraru found LD 350-1 in the Afar region of Ethiopia, some 2.8 million to 2.75 million years from today; it combines the original characteristics of the southern apes (e.g., partial teeth) with those of later humans (small teeth, degenerative hips). The value of this material lies in pushing known personal records to an earlier date than in declaring the birth of a species. [1][2]

Fossilgeologial age was determined by the comparison of the site with the regional ash of the region, and Villamoare and others reported 2.80-2.75 Ma in 2015 in Science. Attribution is determined by comparison between the lower jaw and the teeth, rather than by a single measurement. Common misunderstandings

A common misconception is to treat one of the earliest Fossils as the birth day of an entire human being, or to mix “personal” with “smart”. LD 350-1 is only a candidate for the first known person; the earlier group may not have left a identifying Fossil. Nor can it be inferred from the discovery of stones that the producers necessarily belong to the human race, since many earlier human beings had coexisted in the same area. [1 [2][3]
