Eye Blink

Machine-assisted draft · native review pending

Blinking is a rapid closing and opening of the eyelids, which usually lasts from 0.1 to 0.4 seconds at a single time, often representing 0.3 seconds. It's continuoustime and repeat frequency, not an absolute moment.

What is it?

Blinking is a quick closing of the eyelids and then opening, and a single continuoustime is usually between 0.1 and 0.4 seconds, often representing 0.3 seconds. [1][2] It is both a protective reflection and an unconscious biological rhythm; it is discussed here as “how long a blink lasts”, not “when a man blinks”.

How?

High-speed cameras, eye graphs and video frames can be analysed so that the eyes can be closed and open. A high-speed video study in 2025 measured an average of 284.5 milliseconds (ms) of blinking time for volunteers and a range of 100 to 400 milliseconds of past experiments. [1] Another 2021 clinical study described normal blinking as 0.3 to 0.4 seconds each, with a frequency of approximately 10 to 15 times per minute. [2] There are differences in sampling rates between the two methods and the average given.

Value and Measurement condition

0.1 to 0.4 seconds is the natural blinking common span, completely closed and not completely closed, random blinking and spontaneous blinking should be counted separately. [1][2] Only about 7.5 per cent of times are closed, and the vast majority of time eyes are open, calculated at 15 times a minute and 0.3 seconds a time. Measurement also indicates whether the testee is looking at the screen because its use changes the blink frequency and completeness. [2]

How long does this last, not when?

The blinking time is a continuoustime of a single move and the blinking frequency is “how often”, neither of which is an absolute point of time. Saying “about 0.3 seconds blinking” does not mean that everyone blinks in the same second.

Common misunderstandings

One of the misunderstandings is that blinking is considered a purely discretionary act; most blinking is automatically triggered by the nervous system and can only be partially suppressed if needed. [1][2] There is also a misunderstanding that only concerns about frequency and ignores the length of time: blinking may become short and incomplete when dry eyes or looking at fatigue, and the number alone will omit this change. [2]

Relationship with other scale

An eye blink of about 0.3 seconds, about one third of a heartbeat (0.8 seconds), one order of magnitude less than a breath (about 4 seconds). Hundreds of millions of blinks can be counted 15 times a minute in a person ' s lifetime; these short cycles add up to form an ongoing biological background in sobertime. [1][2]

The time unit of this article and the conversion is based on BIPM for second definiation. [3]

Read Deep I: Measurement condition

The core value of this article is 0.3 s, which is the number of natural blinking single-time closed-opening times with 0.3 seconds of representation. The value is derived from the existing review chain of this article and is not re-estimated by title; it is marked as approximate. [1] Once a change is made, the reading changes, so the body text always writes the unit, the reference and the condition.

Conditions are recorded as: population = healthy adults; blinkType = natural, spontaneous blinking; measurement = high-speed video or eye-recording eye closed — open process; typicalRange = 100-400 ms; representative Value = 300 ms. These conditions determine whether it can be compared directly with another reading; for example, changing the diameter to a radius and changing the instantaneous value to an adult average would change the order of magnitude and not just the size of the number. [2]

Read Deep II: Error and soundary

When placed in the scale ladder, humans are compared (1.7). This article is not a simple multiple relationship to it, but two anchors that differ under the same measure; unit and reference are first harmonized and then the larger and smaller are discussed. [3]

Error and baseary: Unsegregated values are derived mainly from medical selection and sample assumptions. If a cross-entry comparison is to be made, the original significant figures should be retained, without adding the results to the precision that the instrument does not support; the textual interpretation of this article and the scene description should be used only to create a scale sense and not to replace a medical record.
