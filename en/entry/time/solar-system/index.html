Formation of the Solar System

Machine-assisted draft · native review pending

About 4.6 billion years ago, gas and dust gradually formed the Sun and rows of Galaxy. The original planetary discs, the temperature differences and the collisions congregate explained the formation of different members, leaving small objects as clues to early history.

From a cloud to a plate.

About 4.6 billion years ago, a dense cloud of interstellar gas and dust collapsed into a rotating solar nebula. NASA describes nearby supernova shock waves as one of the possible triggers and should be retained as “possible”, which cannot be written as the only reason for being locked. The central material continues to accumulate, and the surroundings form a plate structure that provides an environment for the formation of the Sun and rows of Galaxy. [1][2]

The center is moving towards the outer edge.

The central region accumulates a large amount of material in a gravitational function, which eventually results in a sun capable of releasing energy through hydrogen fusion. The materials on the outer edge of the disk were not transformed into eight completed planets at the same time, but rather experienced reunification and collision, with some objects growing up and others becoming smaller residual objects. The sun, planets and small objects are the result of different processes and cannot be treated as a model in an instant line. [2]

Temperature makes the distribution of materials different

Temperature near the young sun is high and heat-resistant rock materials can be left behind; more distant environments allow more ice and gas to participate in planetary formation. This helps to explain the difference between inner-terrestrial and outer-side giants. Thus, the space layout of the solar system is not only a matter of distance but also preserves information about the formation of the environment. When presenting the original planetary disk, the colour of the material should be understood as an indication of such differences in conditions. [1][2]

Why would a small object tell the past?

No part of the material that has been assembled into the planets has become objects of asteroids, comets, etc. NASA Space Place stressed that studying the legacy of these formations could help to understand the origins of the solar system. They are not all the same: by comparing different members in the same formation framework, they can ask which substances are being preserved, which have undergone subsequent changes, and how lines are being assembled. [1][2]

Conversion does not increase the precision of the original geologic age, nor does it mean that all the planets were in current orbit at that time. This point points to the stage of formation, the mechanisms of animated expression of plates, collisions and orbits, rather than the real reconstruction of one day. [1]

Meteorite fixed years of “formation” to measurable age

The oldest solids in the solar system include implacable packagings in calcium-rich aluminium packages (CAI) and spherical particles; leadiisotope and short-lived radioactive nuclides give about 4567 million years of age. [1][2] This age is bound by the condensation of the first solids in the solar nebula, not the moment when the Sun itself ignites hydrogen fusion. The “formation of the solar system” is written as a date, and the type of sample measured must be specified.

It's not a line.

The dust from the original planetary disk is first assembled into stars, which then grow into planets through collisions and gravitational disturbances; some collisions destroy existing celestial bodies and others merge. [2] The final orbit and size of the planet are therefore the product of multiple events and cannot be pushed back today ' s eight-planet layout into instantaneous initial conditions. The lifetime of the disk is about millions of years, and planetary assembly is likely to last longer.

Five-axis Contrast

The solar system formed about 4.6 billion years ago on the time axis; on the space axis, the planetary area was about 60 au; on the mass axis, the sun accounted for more than 99 per cent of the total mass of the system; and on the temperature axis, the temperature gradient in the disc determined the distribution of rock and ice. Age, scale, mass and temperature are bound to model. [1][2]

Meteorite gave the event time.

The solar system forms about 4.567 billion years, mainly given by the CAI lead isotope, which is the first condensed solid, but the Sun itself may be hundreds of thousands of years earlier. There is a slight difference between the ages given in the different meteorite and isotope systems.
