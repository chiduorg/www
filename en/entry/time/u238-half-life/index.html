Uranium-238 Half-Life

Machine-assisted draft · native review pending

The half-life of uranium-238 is approximately 44.68 billion years. It shows how long half of this nuclide decay takes, is the nuclide's lifetime, is not the moment of a universe incident, and does not mean that uranium-238 will disappear as a whole on a given day.

What is it?

U-238 is the vast majority of natural uranium, with 92 protons and 146 neutrons. It has evolved through alphadecay into a series of nuclides, such as thorium-234, radon-226, leading to lead-206. half-life refers to the time required for the decay of half of the uranium-238 atom nuclear batch, approximately 44.68 billion years. [1] This is the lifetime of a nuclide and answers “how long is half of this nuclide decay” rather than “when an object or geological event occurred”.

How?

Such a long half-life cannot be verified directly by waiting for a cycle. Researcher Measurement calculates the number of alpha particles in a unit of time in a sample of highly enriched uranium, and then determines the number of U-238 atoms in the sample using methods such as mass spectrometry, thus extrapolating the decay constant and half-life. The IEA review adopted the 1971 precisionmeasurement for highly enriched uranium and gave a recommended value of 4.468 ± 0.005 billion years. [1] Britannica ' s general intestry writing is approximately 4.5 billion years, noting that uranium-238 represents more than 99% of natural uranium. [2] The two are different in precision, but identical in order of magnitude.

Value and Measurement condition

At 31,557,600 seconds per year, 4,468 million years is about 1.41×10^17seconds, or over 140 kyotz seconds. [1] This figure corresponds to about one third of the current age of universe and is slightly older than the Earth's; it is not a nuclide life “over universe”. Uranium-238 still exists in the Earth ' s crust, suggesting that a half-life is only halved, not all. In the discussion of geological fixed years, “half-life” and “sampling age” should be separated: the latter is calculated by the parent-substance ratio and the closure conditions. [1][2]

Common misunderstandings

One misconception is that 44.68 billion years are considered “the age of uranium-238” or “the moment of the universe events”. half-life is not a starttime, nor does it mean that all uranium-238 will disappear simultaneously after 44.68 billion years. Another misconception is to confuse half-life with the survival of a single atom nuclear time: when a single nuclear is random is decay, and half-life only describes statistical trends in large quantities of nuclides. There is also information that combines “about 4.5 billion years” and “446.8 billion years” with excess decimals, in effect respecting each other's accuracy. [1][2]

Relationship with other scale

The half-life of uranium-238 is much longer than the 5,700 years of carbon-14 and is also longer than human civilization and human life and is therefore suitable for studying ancient rock and minerals since the Earth was formed. [1][2] It is nearly twenty times longer than the age of Galaxy (approximately 23 billion years), but still less than the age of universe. It's wrong to compare it to "How many seconds after Big Bang": it's a nuclide scale, measuring how long it lasts, not when it happens.
