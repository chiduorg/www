What is it?

“Now” is a common benchmark for the station's time axis, taking the age of universe to about 13.8 billion years. It is not a physical clock reading, but rather a reference point where the universiteime from Big Bang and the time from Earth's history are placed at the same coordinates. Today's universe has evolved from an incendiary, even early state to Star, Galaxy, Planet and unverse. It is also the benchmark for all "how many years from now" entryconversion to UNiverse. [1][2]

How?

Universe age is assumed by observation and model. Experiments such as Planck have tamperature and oscillation, limiting the composition and expansion of universe and inverting age under standard universe studies model; the result is observationerror and model conditions. [1] Britannica summarises the age of universe at about 13.8 billion years; NASA Space Place gives the solar system about 4.6 billion years ago to be used to map the distance of the Earth ' s history. [2] [3]

Time Coordinates and Measurement condition

This station uses 13.8 billion years of conversion as approximately 4.354×10¹⁷ seconds as a full library T0 benchmark; this is round reference, and the bottom does not represent measurement precision. Universe age, solar system age, and human history, geological age, using different years, cannot be directly reduced to the same precision. Seconds don't increase with reality. [1][3]

Common misunderstandings

The common misconception is to consider “now” as the absolute central moment of unverse, or to consider 13.8 billion years as accurate to the year. Estimates of universe age still show a few percentages of uncertainty, with models giving slightly different values from data fairs. [1][2] Another misunderstanding is to interpret “light from far away galaxy” as seeing that galaxy “at this moment”; what we see is the light from the past.

Relationship to other phases

Now, the first generation of Big Bang, Composite, Star and Galaxy form, and the next ununiverse future; it is also a benchmark that places 4.6 billion years of the solar system, 300,000 years of humanity on the same axis as 37 years of the World Wide Web.
