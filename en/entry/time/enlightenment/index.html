The Enlightenment

Machine-assisted draft · native review pending

Enlightenment usually refers to European ideological movements centred on reason, experience, criticism and reform from the late 17th to the 18th century; around 1685, it was only close to Newton’s “Theory” (1687) and the “Reference” of the Glory Revolution (1688).

What is it?

Enlightenment usually refers to an ideological movement in Europe from the late seventeenth to the eighteenth centuries, centred on using reason, experience and criticism to understand the world and drive change. Britannica summed it up as a European knowledge movement from the 17th to the 18th century, involving the realignment of the concepts of God, reason, nature and humanity; historians often used the glorious revolution of 1688 to the French revolution of 1789 as a broader time framework, while the fast-track facts gave a range of about 1601 to 1800 years. [1] It is not an event in a particular country, but a transnational network of books, communications and discussions.

How?

Evidence comes from published books, periodicals, encyclopedias, letters, Sharon records and university courses. The Stanford Philosophy Encyclopaedia states that at the heart of the 18th century was the activities of the French “Truths” community, including Voltaire, Dedero, Mendezo and others, also extended to Scotland, Germany and the Americas; the Encyclopaedia represents these cooperative projects. The Mathematics of Natural Philosophy, published by Newton in 1687, provides a powerful example of “the understanding of nature in mathematics and observation”. [3]

Common misunderstandings

The common misconception is to equate the Enlightenment Movement with an anti-religious or mono-State movement. Enlightened thinkers have different positions on religion, nature and society, as do France, Scotland, Germany and the Americas. [1][2] Another misunderstanding is to regard it as a purely optimistic rational worship; to enlighten internal debate about suspicion, empirism and opposition to abstract systems, which was later challenged by romanticism and political violence. [1]

Relationship to other phases

Before the Enlightenment Movement, the Renaissance of Arts, the Scientific Revolution and the Dissemination of Printing Arts followed by the Industrial Revolution, the Political Revolution and the Modern System of Science. It expands public discourse to a space where authority can be criticized, but does not create industrialization or democracy alone. [1][2] The Enlightenment places greater emphasis on testable experience and public reason than the medieval philosophy; it believes more in universal rational communication than in the romanticism of the nineteenth century.
