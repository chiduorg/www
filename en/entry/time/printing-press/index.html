The Printing Press

Machine-assisted draft · native review pending

This article refers to the printing of European metalworking machines, often using as a reference the work done in Gutenberg in 1440; in the 11th century, China Baixuan used mud in the 11th century, and in the 14th century there was a hard print in Korea.

What is it?

This entry is about metallic machine printing in Europe, usually based on Gutenberg's work in the mid-15th century. Live print is printed in a single movable font template, with ink, paper and press-printed text; it is a different technical route from sculpture printing and handwritten copying. [1] It needs to be made clear first that paper and paper printing are in China earlier and that the first books available in live print are in Korea in the 14th century. [1]

How?

Gutenberg's living records show that he was sold to a goldsmith in 1436, that the contract in 1438 involved the construction of a wood stamper, that his printing experiment was quite mature around 1450 and that the famous 42 Bibles were completed in 1455. [2] According to information from the National Antiquity Protection Centre of China, during the calendar year of North Song Song (1042-1048), civilians invented mud-printing techniques, approximately 400 years earlier than in Gutengburg, where the book was printed in live print; the writing, layout, hard-copy and printing processes were documented in Dream Creek. [3]

Common misunderstandings

A common misconception is that Gutenberg invented the print of live words. The actual and earlier Chinese mudheading and Korean printbacks are not unique to Europe; the key contribution of Gutenberg is the combination of metal work, ink and press presses into a reusable European printing system. [1][2][3] There is also a misconception that the print revolution is to be completed overnight; handwritten books, sculptor books and living books have long coexisted, and the literacy rate has increased for hundreds of years.

Relationship to other phases

Live print is preceded by text, paper and hand-written distribution, followed by Renaissance, Religious Reform, Scientific Revolution and Enlightenment; it reduces the cost of copying texts and allows criticism and debate to spread more quickly across regions. [1][3] Printing is still a physical technology that requires workshops, metals and paper compared to today ' s digital network, but it has transformed information reproduction from a work of a few bookkeepers into a scalable industry.

It's not all about the print revolution.

The working words can be reused, but typesetting, proofreading and binding are still labour-intensive; the cost of the early printing plant comes mainly from paper, ink and labour. [1][3] Embossed printing is appropriate for fixed text and images, and live words are suitable for multiple reordering; both technologies coexist over time. The print revolution is attributed only to one invention, the living word, which ignores the other links in the production chain.

Technology, materials and communication networks are working together to change the book market. [1][3]

Metal working and whole print

The key in Gutenberg is not “invented living words”, but rather a replicable production system that combines metal, ink, printing and layout; China already has mud, wood and metal. The European metallic work co-operates with the alphabet system, paper supply and market demand. Body text's “printing” is the node of the technology system, not the invention of a single person.
