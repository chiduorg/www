Temperature at the base of Earth's mantle

Machine-assisted draft · native review pending

The bottom of the mantle is near the nuclear mantle. High pressure experiments give about 3,570 ± 200 K of the olive rock solid phase, which can be considered as the upper limit reference for the site's mantle; it is not the average temperature of the entire mantle, nor the temperature of the core.

What is it?

The mantle lies between the crust and the Earth's core and is the largest circle of the Earth's volume. The layer close to the nuclear mantle is the highest and most stressful part of the mantle. Nomura et al., 2014 high-pressure experiments set the solid phase line of the olive rock system at approximately 3,570 ± 200 K, which leads to the assumption that the surface of the mantle near the nuclei baseary will not significantly exceed this range. [1] This page uses 3,570 K as the upper limit reference for “temperature on the bottom of the mantle”, rather than writing the whole mantle as this temperature.

How?

This number comes from the lab instead of the temperature device. The researchers used high pressure devices to press samples of the olive rock composition to pressure near the nucleotosphere baseary, while heating and judging when the melting began using X-ray distillation and three-dimensional imaging; the solid phase is the temperature line “to begin melting”. [1] Since the base of the mantle is not global melting, the actual temperature should be below or close to this solid phase, it gives a cap constraint. [1] The presentation by USGS on the interior of the Earth provides the background of the layer structure: the mantle is the solid rock layer, which is below the liquid outer core. [2]

Value and Measurement condition

3,570 K approximation 3,297 °C. It describes the solid line of olive rock under the pressure of the nuclear mantle, not the average of the mantle, nor the temperature of the earth's core. The top of the mantle is much colder near the crust, and the temperature rises with depth; using 3,570 K as a number of "teremperature" will wipe out the huge gradient of about 2,900 km thick. [1]

Common misunderstandings

A common misconception is that the mantle is used as a liquid magma. Most of the mantle is solid rock, which can move slowly over long geological periods; only local areas are close to or reach the melting point. [2] Another misconception is to confuse the bottom of the mantle with the temperature: the core is dominated by iron nickel, and the temperature and melting are another set of problems, and the entry has been dedicated to pure iron melting under kernel base pressure. [1]

Relationship with other scale

On the temperature axis, 3,570 K is above the daily high temperature and basalt melting zone, below approximately 5,772 K on the surface of the Sun and well below the pure iron melting reference point of the Earth's core of about 6,230 K. [1] On space, it carries to the surface about 2,900 kilometres below the surface of the nuclear mantle, and on the time, the mantle traverse and mountain-building process removes this heat to the surface for tens of millions of years.

The othermodynamicsteperature unit of this article is based on BIPM's definition for Kelvin. [3]

Iron melting point

The normal pressure melting point for pure iron is approximately 1,811 K, or 1,538 °C. This value is pure eliment iron and does not directly indicate the complete melting of steel, biomass or high-pressure subterranean steel containing carbon and other element.
