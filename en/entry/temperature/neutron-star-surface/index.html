Cassiopeia A neutron-star surface reference

Machine-assisted draft · native review pending

The neutron in Queen's A remains gives a concrete example of the surface. Chandra's research notes describe it as an order of magnitude near 2 million degrees; this is from X-ray observation and atmospheric model analysis, rather than setting all neutron stars to the same temperature.

What is it?

A is a young supernova in the galaxy, with a neutron at the center. The outermost layer of the neutron is a thin carbon atmosphere of about four inches thick, which is constricted by very strong surface gravity. The surfaces that we see are the effective temperature of the atmosphere when it is exposed to outward radiation, not the temperature within the entire star. A summary of Chandra describes it as almost 2 million degrees of magnitude. [1] This makes it a specific sample of young neutron cooling.

How or how?

Chandra observation is a hot X-ray spectrum, not a picture that can be read directly. By comparing the spectrum with the models of different atmospheric components, researchers found that carbon atmosphere models explain observation and explain why the star does not have a visible X-ray pulse. [2] Under carbon-atmospheric model, the radiation area can cover the entire star surface, so there is no need to assume a small hot spot. temperature, radius and distance enter the same assembly process; changes in atmospheric composition and extrapolation of temperature will also change. What is here is the surface effectiveness on which Model relies, rather than the contact mode.

Value and Measurement condition

This article uses approximately 2×10⁶ K as the order of magnitude coordinates of the star ' s surface valid for typologies, and retains a significant figures. It is not the core or the brightness of the temperature; the brightness of the temperature relies on the observation band and radiation mechanisms, while the effective temperature describes total radiation per unit area. Transparency in the carbon atmosphere, surface gravity, distance and year-to-year observation affect the intended outcome. [1][2] The star is still cooling, so the value corresponds to a specific observation period rather than to a constant that is constant forever.

Common misunderstandings

First, the surface is treated as the average temperature of the whole star; the inner neutron is much hotter than the surface, but the internal temperature can only be indirectly bound by cooling model. Second, use2×10⁶ K as a common value for all neutron stars; the neutron stars of different ages, atmospheric composition and magnetic field have different surfaces. Thirdly, the colour of X-rays should be considered directly as temperature; it must also consider atmospheric intransparentness, partial response and instrumentation. Fourth, the carbon atmosphere is understood as an ordinary gas; it is compressed under extremely heavy gravity and is very different in nature from the Earth ' s atmosphere. [2]

Why is the carbon atmosphere important?

Neutron in quarkers A is covered by a thin layer of carbon atmosphere; atmospheric constituents decide not to be transparent and spectral, thereby changing the proposed combination of effective temperature and radius. [1][2] If the hydrogen atmosphere is assumed, the same X-ray data may give different combinations of temperature and radius. Atmospheric model is not decorative, but part of the measurement.

Why is the young neutron hotter?

The neutron star was born of a core collapse supernova, with a very high initial internal temperature, followed by cooling through neutrons and surface radiation. [1] quarkers A is only about 350 years old, so the surface can still be about 2×10⁶ K; the older pulsar surface will be much colder. The surface is determined by age, atmosphere and magnetic field.

Five-axis Contrast

On the temperature axis, the surface is about 2×10⁶ K; on the space axis, the neutron star is about 10 km in radius; on the mass axis it is about 1.4 solar mass; and on thetime axis it is cooling. The surface is just a little bit on the cooling curve. [1][2]
