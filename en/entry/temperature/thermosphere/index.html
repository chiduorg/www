Temperature / Mid temperature

Thermospheric gas kinetic-temperature reference

Machine-assisted draft · native review pending

The aerodynamics of thin thermosphere gases are usually around 500-2,000 K, where 1,000 K is taken as an example. It describes the mean kinetic energy of the gas particles, which is not the equivalent of the temperature that must have been reached by the object at that altitude.

What is it?

Thermosphere is an area in the Earth ' s atmosphere that extends from approximately 90 km to 500-1,000 km and is above the middle layer and below the outer surface. [2][3] The gas here is extremely thin, and temperature is usually described by the aerodynamics temperature: its average kinetic energy for gaseous particles is typical for 500-2,000 K, and 1,000 K is just an example on the axis. [1] It is not an entire layer of "equivalent thermal gas" or the ionosphere itself.

How?

Thermperature cannot be directly exposed to measurement by ordinary typerature. Common tools include satellite resistance inversion, mass spectrometers and the in situ measurement of the Ramuillet probe, incoherent dispersible radars and engineering atmospheric models based on these data. [1][2] NASA's low-orbit spacecraft charge design standards write neutral gas kinetic temperatures 500-2,000 K and give the corresponding range of environmental pressure as project input. [1] These results are usually functions of altitude, latitude, local time and solar activity, rather than a constant.

Value and Measurement condition

1000 K conversion is 1,000 - 273.15 = 726.85 °C, approximately 727 °C. [1] However, “727 °C” cannot be directly understood to mean that the air at that altitude heats objects like ovens: very low thermal density, low particle impact and limited heat delivery to objects. Temperature also rises with increased solar activity, with differentials in day, night and latitude, and therefore more commonly used in engineering with model profiles rather than single readings.

Common misunderstandings

“Thermosphere 1,000 K so that the satellite is burned to 1,000 K” confuses the gasokinetic temperature with the object balance temperature; very little heat can be passed from thin gases. The second misunderstanding is to equate the thermosphere with the ionosphere: the space overlaps, but the thermosphere is divided by the temperature structure and the ionosphere by the degree of ionization. [2][3] Third, the thermperature is considered to have been elevated with height; it actually rises faster in the lower layer, and it tends to flatten higher and is subject to solar activity. Finally, 1,000 K is not " a measured value at a certain altitude at a given date ".

Relations with other temperate areas

1000 K above the average surface of Venus of approximately 737 K and above the melting point of aluminium 933.473 K, but below the iron of approximately 1,811 K and much below the solar surface of approximately 5,772 K. It's more than 700 K above the Earth's surface, but because the gas density is extremely low, it is not possible to convert the temperature value directly into a "hotness" or "heating capacity". Thermosphere just indicates that temperature is a measure of the average kinetic energy of molecules and that the heat transfer depends on the amount of matter.

Venus mean surface-temperature reference
