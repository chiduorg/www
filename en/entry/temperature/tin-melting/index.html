Tin melting point

Machine-assisted draft · native review pending

Pure tin has a melting point of approximately 505.08 K (231.93 °C) under constant pressure and is one of the common low melting point metals. Tin also has alkyl transformation and “tin disease”; welding, copper and tin plating are not directly equivalent to pure tin melting points.

What is it?

Tin is a metal marked Sn with softer texture and density of about 7.3 g/cm3. The melting point of pure tin under constant pressure, i.e. the transformation of the whole white tin crystal into a liquid, is discussed here, not the melting of welding, copper or tin plating steel. [1][2] Pure tin is melted at 231.93 °C and conversion is 505.08 K. The low melting point of tin makes it widely used in welding, copper alloys and tin plating. [3]

How?

Pure tin melting points can be used for heat analysis, scanning heat, electrical resistance changes and high temperature radiation temperature measurements. Samples need to avoid oxidation and ingestion, as surface oxidation affects heat exposure and reading. WebElements lists 505.08 K, RSC lists 505.078 K, Britannica uses 231.97 °C. [1][2][3] Very small differences, mainly from unit conversion and end circle.

Value and Measurement condition

505.08 K corresponds to 231.93 °C, security condition is a solid-liquid balance of pure tin, approximately 1 atm, and no significant impurities. [1] Tin is also in the vicinity of approximately 13.2 °C with an alien transformation of white and grey tin, which is a solid case change, not a melting process; it is an error to describe “tin disease” as tin melting. [3] Welding is usually tin lead or tin copper alloys, with a solid phase, a liquid phase, and pure tin; copper is copper tin alloys, and the melting zone is dominated by copper content.

Common misunderstandings

First, welding's 183 °C cosmopolitan temperature as a pure tin melting point; both components are different. Secondly, the disease is considered to be melting; it is a transformation from white to grey at low temperatures. Third, write 231.93 °C as 505.08 °C, or mix Kelvin with degree Celsius. Fourth, all tin-containing materials are considered to melt at the same temperature, ignoring alloy composition and plating matrix constraints. [1][3]

Relations with other temperate areas

K is below the zinc melting point 692.68 K and the lead melting point is about 600 K and below the boiling point of water 373 K and above 132 K; it is above the room temperature of approximately 293 K. Pure tin melting points are about 49 K higher than welding 183 C and about 670-770 K lower than copper 900-1000 C liquid phase. [1] [3] This means that the “tin melting point” only describes pure tin and does not represent all tin-containing engineering materials.
