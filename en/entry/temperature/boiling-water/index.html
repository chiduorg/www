Boiling water

Machine-assisted draft · native review pending

In the vicinity of standard atmospheric pressure, water boils at about 100 °C (373.15 K). Boiling point changes with pressure: lower pressure boils with lower pressure and higher pressure with higher pressure.

It's not just the water.

Water molecules can enter the gas phase from the liquid surface; boiling is associated with the formation and growth of internal bubbles in the liquid. IAPWS explains the process with vapour pressure: when the vapour pressure of water reaches the surrounding pressure, conditions for boiling are available. Therefore, the boiling point was from the beginning the physical properties of the temperature together with the pressure. [1]

Why is it written here about 100 degrees C?

This page uses the standard atmospheric pressure of 101.325 kPa for daily reference around 100°C, or about 373.15 K. It is suitable for comparing water and other substances, but it is not an exact reading that can be established at any time when pressure changes; nor does it mean that day-to-day material has the same precision. [1][2]

How do altitude and pressure change results?

Air pressure around higher elevations is usually low, and the vapour pressure of water is balanced at lower temperature; the pressure environment is the opposite. When observing the water in both containers, even if it is boiling, it cannot be concluded that they are the same. For comparison to be repeated, pressure and temperature measurements should be recorded simultaneously. [1]

Temperature meets conditions and bubbles need to start forming.

IAPWS also states that bubble formation requires a nuclear process; when suitable nuclear conditions are not in place, liquids may be above normal boiling points without significant boiling. This explains why “a number has reached” and “a sustained bubble” is not exactly the same thing, nor can they estimate the real number of bubbles in animated. [1]

Thus, when comparing the boiling point in the table, it is possible to check whether it uses an atmospheric pressure or other pressure, and then whether the numbers are daily approximations or precision thermometers. The mere addition of decimal places without such information would make two otherwise non-controversial records appear to be conflicting. [1]

How does it fit with the precision thermometer?

High-precision information must further distinguish between the temperature-column Measurement condition: the standard atmospheric pressure water boiling point given by IAPWS is approximately 99.974°C on ITS-90. Water does not change the nature as a result; rather, the temperature criteria are more refined. This page retains a comparative anchor point of about 100 °C; data under clear temperature should be used for research or marking. [1]

Boiling point changes with pressure

The standard atmospheric pressure is about 100 °C boiling; the high altitude air pressure is low, the boiling point is lower and the altitude or pressure is higher. The pressure pot heats the water up to 110 °C, using this. When “water boils at 100°C”, pressure conditions must be given at the same time, otherwise they are only daily approximations.

It's not heating up.

Turning water from 100 °C liquid to 100 °C steam requires the absorption of large amounts of water, but no rise in temperature. [1] This is one of the reasons why steam burns are more severe than hot water at the same time as the temperature: the latent heat is released when the steam condensation occurs. Boiling point describes the case change condition, not the sum of the head.

Five-axis Contrast

On the temperature axis, the constant boiling point is approximately 373.15 K; on the space axis, the water molecule enters the gas phase; on the mass axis, the carving latent heat is associated with the inter-molecular hydrogen key; and on the time axis, the boiling requires nuclear and continuous heating. Temperature, pressure and phase commondefinition boiling point. [1][2]

Boiling Point, Ratent Head and Pressure

The water is boiling at 100 °C, but the boiling point changes with the air pressure: the high altitude is about 90 °C and the pressure pan can reach 120 °C. The carburetor latent heat keeps the water hotter at the boiling point, which is why the steam burns are more severe. 100 °C of body text must be subject to the condition of “standard atmospheric pressure”. [1][S2]

Read-ment convention and conversion

The quantity used in this article is approximately 373.15 K (100 °C) and the measure convention is water-normal-boiling-temperature-reference. Temperature is the strength, and readings depend on the thermometer, the point and the motheral equilibrium; different parts of the same object can vary at different times. When comparing this reading to the adjacententry, replace it with the same unit and then take the margin; do not reduce it directly with a different value. If there is only one thing to remember: the answer is “the object probably falls on which order of magnitude”, it is not a constant that can replace all samples.
