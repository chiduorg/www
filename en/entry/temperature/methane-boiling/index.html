Methane normal boiling point

Machine-assisted draft · native review pending

The normal boiling point for pure methane at 1 atm is approximately 111.7 K (−161.5 °C). Differences in data from 111 K to -162 °C are derived from rounding and data selection; liquefied natural gas is a mixture and cannot be directly applied to pure methane values.

What is it?

Methane is the simplest of the hydrocarbons, which are usually non-colour-free gases under pressure; it is liquefied at about 111.7 K. This is a normal boiling point for pure methane, not a single boiling point for natural gas, or for methane combustion. The melting point of methane under 1 atm is approximately −182.5 °C (90.65 K), so the constant pressure stabilization temperature zone of liquid pure methane is approximately 90.7-111.7 K. [3]

How?

The boiling point is determined by the saturation vapour pressure balance with the external pressure: placement of a pure methane sample in a constant temperature bath, measurement liquid - the vapour pressure when the gas is balanced, finds a vapour pressure equal to 101.325 kPa. The NIST list of cryogenic data shows methane as 111.7 K (−161.5 °C); NIST WebBook's case change data gives 111 ± 2 K, the average of 13 measration values; Britannica gives -162 °C. The difference between [1][2][3] is only about 0.5 K, mainly from round and raw data selection. Measurement also requires a sufficient number of uncondensed gases, such as nitrogen, in the sample, otherwise the total pressure will be lifted by impurity fraction pressure and the reading boiling point will shift.

Value and Measurement condition

111.7 K minus 273.15 plus -161.45 °C, NIST Table is written in a single decimal number to -161.5 °C, Britannica to −162 °C. [1][3] ±2 K of 111 ± 2 K is the data discrete range, not the fixed error of each event. [2] Boiling points shall be subject to pressure; methane boils at lower temperature at lower pressure and the pressure increases. Liquefied natural gas has reorganization points such as ethane, propane, which usually have more bubble points than pure methane, and cannot be used as a storage for all Liquefied natural gas.

Common misunderstandings

First, natural gas is equated with pure methane; the components of the mixture have different boiling points, and storage design is based on composition. [2][3] Second, use boiling points as “temperature required for combustion”; methane ignition and flame temperature are another type of problem, with common-pressure boiling points describing only case change. Third, read 111.7 K to 111.7 °C; the difference between the two 273.15 K. Fourth, the low boiling point is considered to be equal to the high heat value; the mass is different from the temperature value of volume. Fifth, mix the pressure of the tank with the boiling point; the tank is close to the normal pressure, but evaporation changes the gas phase composition and bubble point.

Relations with other temperate areas

111.7 K above oxygen 90.2 K and nitrogen 77.4 K, lower than ammonia 239.8 K and water 373.15 K. [1][3] It is much warmer than liquid hydrogen 20.3 K, so that the amount of refrigeration required for liquefied natural gas is less than liquefied hydrogen, but it is still covered by cryogenic engineering. Compared to methane melting point 90.65 K, constant-pressure liquid methane is present only in a narrow temperature zone of about 21 K. [3]
