Liquid oxygen normal boiling reference

Machine-assisted draft · native review pending

The normal boiling point for pure oxygen at 1 atm is about 90.2 K (−183.0 °C). This is gas-base changereference of liquid balance, not any liquid oxygen storage tanks, pressure, or operating conditions; both the purity of the sample and the pressure must be specified.

What is it?

Liquid oxygen is a light blue liquid formed when oxygen cools to about 90.2 K under 1 atm. [1][3] The term “liquid oxygen” here usually refers to the normal boiling point of pure oxygen, i.e. gas-liquid balance, rather than reading at any point in the tank. Oxygen condensates under constant pressure at approximately 54.8 K, so liquid oxygen is stable only in a narrow temperature zone of about 54.8 K to 90.2 K; below the condensation point becomes solid oxygen. [1][3]

How?

Measurement at normal boiling point is the placement of a pure oxygen sample in a pressureable container, where the two-phase gaseous fluids reach their mothermal equilibrium, and the search for saturated vapour pressure corresponds to the tymperature of 101.325 kPa. [1][2] NIST Chemischary WebBook's database chart Tboil is 90.2 K, with an indication of 0.2 K as composite uncertainty; NIST's cryogenic technology table lists oxygen as 902 K (−183.0 °C); and Britannica's nature table similarly gives -183.0 °C. [1][2][3] The information is based on a constant pressure limit for pure oxygen rather than industrial liquid oxygen containing argon and nitrogen.

Value and Measurement condition

90.2 K minus 273.15 −182.95 °C. The information is written in one decimal −183.0 °C. The difference is round. [1][2][3] 90.2 K is an approximation, and 0.2 K uncertainty cannot be considered a fixeder for each sample. Boiling point changes with external pressure: Boil point drops below pressure of 101.325 kPa and rises when pressure is pressed; storage tanks often operate slightly above normal pressure to reduce evaporation, so that temperature does not necessarily equal 902 K. [1] The three phase points for oxygen are 54.33 K, which is not the same concept as the Condensation Point for Pressure 54.8 K. [1]

Common misunderstandings

First, light blue of liquid oxygen is coloured as a container; the data describe pure oxygen as a light blue liquid below -183 °C, coloured from the oxygen molecule itself. Second, refer to 90.2 K as “close to absolute Zero so it is extremely difficult to obtain”; it is 77.4 K higher than liquid nitrogen, and the air fraction can be produced in large quantities. [2] Third, the normal boiling point is used as a storage box for a type of rocket; the actual propulsion system is also subject to pressure pressure, overcooling and leaking heat. Fourth, expand oxygen in 90 K to "all gases are liquid in 90 K," and the case changeemperature of different gases is very different. [2]

Relations with other temperate areas

90.2 K above liquid nitrogen 77.4 K, below methane 111.7 K, well above liquid hydrogen 20.3 K and liquid helium 4.2 K. [2] It is in the low-temperature zone of the air fraction and is also a constant-pressure hot zone that liquid oxygen needs to be maintained as an oxidant. Compared to the water's 273.15 K ice point, 90.2 K is about one third of the liquid oxygen, indicating that liquid oxygen is typical of low-temperature liquids, rather than daily refrigeration. [1][2]
