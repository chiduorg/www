Deep ocean water temperature

Machine-assisted draft · native review pending

Deep seawater below 2000 metres is usually about 2-4°C, which takes 275 K (approximately 2°C). It is a temperature of deep water, not a sea surface temperature or a local high-temperature fluid of hydrothermal vents.

What is it?

The deep-sea temperature typically has an index of less than 100 metres, a weak or totally non-luminous layer of water. The deep sea is not a unified temperature: less than 200 metres enters the weak ionosphere, less than 1,000 metres is completely lightless; deep seawater is long-term approaching the ice point, often summarized as about 2-4°C. [1][2] This Article takes 275 K (approximately 2°C) for deep seawater below 2000 m. It describes the water mass temperature, not sea surface temperature, or local high-temperature fluid from hydrothermal vents.

How?

Temperature depth, Argo buoy and anchor sensors. Information from NOAA indicates that the surface of the ocean absorbs solar radiation and that deep layers lack heating, resulting in low temperature; on average, the deep sea is written about 4 °C. [1] NOAA ' s information on the thermocline further indicates that temperature is largely constant between approximately 3300 feet and 13100 feet, and that the temperature at a deeper point is close to the ice point. [2] Local differences in different basins, ocean currents and geothermal activities must be recorded together with the depth of the site and water mass.

Value and Measurement condition

275 K equals 1.85 °C, about 2 °C. 2-4 °C is a common generalization of deep seawater, but this is not “the whole deep sea”; there may be different readings in the Mediterranean, polar deep water and near hydrothermal vents. [1][2] The pressure is high and does not change the temperature reading itself; temperature is the strength and does not need to be modified by pressure, as is the case with volume. The depth of the sites, the water mass and the seasons influence the results.

Common misunderstandings

First, the deep sea is treated as a simple extension of the surface of the sea; the temperature leap below is slow but stable. Second, the deep sea is considered to be frozen; seawater salinity and pressure keep its ice point below 0°C and deep water remains liquid. Third, treat 4 °C as a uniform value for all depths; below 2000 metres is usually closer to 2 °C. Fourth, the 350 °C of the hydrothermal vent is considered a deep-sea background temperature; that is the local fluid that appears near the vent.

Relationship to other temperature

About 2 °C higher than 0 °C of water-freezing (a slightly lower ice point for seawater), about 16.9 °C below sea-surface and below human-body-temperature. It is higher than about −10 °C in permafrost, but the medium is different: one is liquid seawater and one is ice-bearing soil. An extreme contrast between deep-sea background water and local hydrothermal fluid can be seen in a row of 350 °C with hydrothermal-vent.

Temperatures separate the surface from the deep.

The surface absorbs sunlight and the deep layer lacks heating; the two are separated from the thermocliposphere, which rapidly declines with depth. [1][2] The depth of the temperature leap changes with latitude, season and current, not a fixed interface. Treating the ocean as an even bucket of water ignores this stratification.

Five-axis Contrast

At the temperature axis, deep water is about 2-4°C; at the space axis, deep-sea pressure increases with depth; at the mass axis, water mass density drives the moderate salt circulation; and at thetime axis, deep water renewal cycles can last for hundreds of years. temperature, salinity and pressure together determine the water mass movement. [1][2]

Read-ment convention and conversion

The amount used in this article is approximately 2 °C (275 K) and the measure convention is temperature below 2,000 m. Temperature is the strength, and readings depend on the thermometer, the point and the motheral equilibrium; different parts of the same object can vary at different times. When comparing this reading to the adjacententry, replace it with the same unit and then take the margin; do not reduce it directly with a different value. If there is only one thing to remember: the answer is “the object probably falls on which order of magnitude”, it is not a constant that can replace all samples.
