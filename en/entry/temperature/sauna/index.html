Sauna room air temperature

Machine-assisted draft · native review pending

Sauna air is often described as 70 - 100 °C, with 80 - 100 °C and relative humidity 10 - 20 % for Finnish-style sauna studies. The article takes 80 °C (353 K) as its representative value, emphasizing that it is an air temperature rather than a skin or stone temperature.

What is it?

The tamperature of sauna air refers to the temperature of dry hot air in the traditional Finnish sauna, near the height of the face, not on the surface of the stone or on the skin. It is commonly reported that sauna air ranges are written at 70 - 100 °C, of which the Finnish sauna study uses 80 - 100 °C with a relative humidity of about 10 - 20%. [1][2] This article takes 80 °C (353 K) as its representative value for comparison with other temperate zones in the same table.

How?

The sauna temperature is given by the temperature meter or thermostat reading in the room, which is usually installed at a distance from the heater and near the height of the seating position. Water poured on the hot stone increases humidity in a short period of time, but the air temperature does not rise to boiling water temperature; on the contrary, increased humidity significantly alters human heat and body perception. [1] The term “80-100 °C” in the study is defined by the termerature of a controlled experiment or on-site agreement that changes the results of the time, ventilation and seat height that an individual actually endures. [2] Therefore, the cation values must be accompanied by an indication of the location and exposure conditions.

Value and Measurement condition

K equals 79.85 °C, about 80 °C. The common range of 70 - 100 °C means that there is a clear layer of sauna in the same room near the top, seat and ground; the ground is usually far below the head. Air is also not the same as the surface of the object: wood stools, metal arms and skin will heat at different rates. Exposure, relative humidity and past health of 5-20 minutes is more likely to determine safety than a single temperature number. [1] The core of the human body is only slightly elevated in sauna and does not approach the air.

Common misunderstandings

First, use sauna air temperature as water temperature; people are exposed to dry hot air and occasional steam, not 80 °C liquid. Secondly, the higher the effect is considered to be the better; the greater the individual tolerance, the resulting dehydration, blood pressure fluctuations and even heat-related injuries. Third, the temperature readings are used as a whole body; there is a strong contrast between air and human tissue and sweat and heat. Fourth, the humidity is ignored; the same is 80 °C, which is more hot and more difficult to disperse.

Relationship to other temperature

80 °C above 40 °C for hot-spring, below 100 °C for boiling-water and about 100 °C for von. It is in a narrow zone where the human body can endure for a short time, but the tissue is still regulated. When you add sauna to boiling-water, it must be emphasized that the former are air and the latter are water; unlike medium, the risks of heat, heat and burns are different.
