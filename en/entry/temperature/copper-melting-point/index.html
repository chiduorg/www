Copper melting reference

Machine-assisted draft · native review pending

The value of copper condensation fixed points in ITS-90 is 1,357.77 K, approximately 1,084.62 °C. It is a thermometer for the balance of high-purity copper solids, not the uniform melting of all copper alloys or industrial furnaces.

What is it?

Pure copper at 1 atm at 1,357.77 K condensed and conversion at 1,084.62 °C. [1][2] This is a temperature-reference that balances a high-purified copper solid phase with a liquid phase and a high-temperature fixed point for the ITS-90 thermometer; it describes pure element, which is not equivalent to the uniform melting of copper alloys, welds or industrial furnaces. [1][3] Copper tin alloy, copper zinc alloy and oxygen-containing copper have their own solid and liquid phase, and their actual melting often occurs in a temperature zone.

How?

Copper fixes are secured by controlled condensation: high-purity copper is fed into graphite or aluminum oxide, fully melted in an inert atmosphere, slowly cooling, and temperature stays on the condensed platform. The standard platinum-resistant temperature measure is inserted into a fixed point container, to be read after the motheral equilibrium. [1][2] The fixed-point table of NIST thermostats lists copper 1,357.77 K, and the table of RSC element lists 1,084.62 °C, which are cross-checked. [1][3] Oxygen, sulphur and thorium impurities in samples change the shape of the platform, so fixed point experiments emphasize high purity and controlled atmosphere.

Value and Measurement condition

1,357.77 K - 273.15 K = 1,084.62 °C. The same value is obtained in reverse conversion. This value is precisely 0.01 K, but it is a reference under the thermometer definition and is not the exact accuracy of any copper. Industrial pure copper conductivity, oxygen content and trace impurities may cause a slight difference in melting behaviour; copper alloys may begin to appear from less than 1,000 °C. [1][3] When comparing copper material, the number, status and method should be specified at the same time as the “melting point” for copper.

Common misunderstandings

Copper pots, copper pipes and wires do not approach melting points because of routine cooking or hot water; there is a real need to focus on softening, oxidizing and mothing rather than melting. It is also incorrect to consider the fixed point of copper as the “ceiling of the copper alloy”: the melting zone of brass and copper is usually lower or wider. [3] Another misconception is that copper is more difficult to melt than iron; pure iron is about 1,811 K, which is higher than copper. Finally, the multidigit decimal of the temperature fixed point does not mean that the normal temperature measure can read the same precision.

Copper falls between precious metal fixes and iron-based high-temperature materials, which is why it is frequently citicized in thermometers, electrical and heat transfer works.

Iron melting point

The normal pressure melting point for pure iron is approximately 1,811 K, or 1,538 °C. This value is pure eliment iron and does not directly indicate the complete melting of steel, biomass or high-pressure subterranean steel containing carbon and other element.
