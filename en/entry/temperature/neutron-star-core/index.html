Neutron-star core requires a specified cooling model

Machine-assisted draft · native review pending

The core of neutron is a site for the study of ultra-confidential substances. observation surface X-rays vary with times, combined with atmosphere, containment and cooling models, to limit the internal thermal state and hyperflow properties; the core actual temperature and ultra-flow critical temperature must be described separately.

What is it?

The core of the neutron is an area of ultra-heavy material left behind by Star ' s collapse, with a radius of about 10 kilometres and density of up to several times the nuclear saturation density. It consists of neutron, proton, electron and possibly superconductive proton, superflow neutron and strange particles. Core temperature determines how neutrinos take their energy and affects the cooling of stars over centuries to tens of thousands of years. The telescope can only see the outer heat X rays and cannot penetrate the core, so the core is a model to answer. [1][2]

How or how?

The observation path is based on a surface heat X-ray spectrum, which is designed to combine surface effects with carbon or hydrogen atmosphere models, and to link readings from different years into cooling curves. The neutron star of Queen's A is a key example: its surface temperature has declined significantly in about a decade. Page et al. suggested that after core neutron enters a 3P2 superflux state, the formation and deficiency of Cooper's pair increases neutrino radiation and temporarily accelerates cooling; and Shternin et al. confirm the downward trend with an independent observation, which binds the superflow threshold. [1][2] Chandra's synthesis links such surfaces to internal cooling processes. [3] The chain relies on atmospheric, convulsive, distance, age and neutron processes, with any ring change changing the extrapolation.

Value and Measurement condition

No object, age and cooling model are specified here and therefore not given to a single core. About 5×10⁸ K and (7-9) ×10⁸K in the literature are the different model bounds of the superflow threshold tamperature, not the flat tamperature of the current core A, or the values common to all neutron stars. The core is also in the process of cooling off of non-othermal equilibrium: temperature varies from radius to radius and may have both ultra-current, superconductive and ordinary nuclear substances. Combining surface effective temperature, gravitational red red, with internal model temperature on the same curve creates a “uniform core” that does not exist. [1 [2][3]

Common misunderstandings

First, the superflow threshold is used as the current core temperature; the critical mass ratio only indicates where a quantum pair occurs, which is not equivalent to the average temperature represented by stars. Second, the surface is treated as the core; the difference between the two is more order of magnitude, or rather different. Third, treat10⁹ K as a generic value; no object, no age, no model, it cannot correspond to any specific object. Fourth, the term “superflow” is understood as a flow of ordinary liquids; it refers to quantum pairs of dense nuclear substances, which are different from routine fluids. [1]
