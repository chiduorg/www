Sirius A effective temperature

Machine-assisted draft · native review pending

Davis et al., combined with SUSI angular diameter, VLTI results and total radiation flux, received effective temperature 9,845 ± 64 K from Sirius A. This value belongs to Star A, which is not part of the hotter white dwarf, Sirius B; temperature is not alone in determining the total light of Star.

What is it?

Sirius A is the blue and white A1V master, the companion star Sirius B is the white dwarf. [2] The term "temperature" here refers only to the whole star of Sirius A as a valid témperature, i.e. the bold témperature, which is converted from the total radiation and angular diameter, not its surface. [1] Sirius A's radius is about 1.71 times the Sun, 25.4 times the Sun, and temperature is just one of these basic parameters. [2]

How?

Davis received 6.048 ± 0.040 millimetric seconds using the Star interferometer of the University of Sydney at the side of Sirius A; combined with total radiation fluxes from published photometry and spectrophotometry, it was pushed to an effective temperature 9,845 ± 64 K. The same study also weighted the average of SUSI and VLTI angle diameter results to indicate that the different interferences give a slightly different central value and that a single result still needs to be cross-checked.

Value and Measurement condition

9,845 K landed in the temperature zone of approximately 7,500-11,000 K for type A stars; Britannica gave about 9,940 K, which is about 95 K, which is the method of metament, the angular diameter and the wholeness, and is not star suddenly warmed or cooled. [1][2][3] ±64 K is only a proposal for a paper that does not contain systemic differences between different information; the difference between 9,845 K and 9,940 K is about 1 per cent, which is a change in the measration system rather than a short-term change. [2]

Common misunderstandings

First, swapping Sirius A with Sirius B's temperature; B is a white dwarf, small volume, high surface, but low overall light. Second, when Sirius is bright, it is considered to be the highest temperature; the brightness is determined by both light and distance, and Sirius is close and bright; the white dwarf is high, but light is small and the overall light is low. Thirdly, effective temperature is considered to be at the same temperature on the surface; A-type stars also have local variations caused by rotation, convection and magnetic field. [1][3]

Relations with other temperate areas

9 845 K is below the model value of the lasagna polar region of about 10,150 K, higher than the Sun surface of about 5,800 K and higher than the red dwarf of about 3,000 K. It's close to the A-B transition of approximately 11,000 K of B-7, but Sirius A is the main serial star, B-7 is the blue superstar, and temperature and light cannot be seen only one. [2] [3]
