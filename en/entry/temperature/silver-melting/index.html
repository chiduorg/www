Silver freezing fixed point

Machine-assisted draft · native review pending

The solid fixed point of high-purity silver is assigned a value of 1,234.93 K at approximately 961.78 °C in ITS-90. It is a temperature reference; the melting zone of an alloy, such as silver, needs to be separately identified by composition.

What is it?

High-purity silver at 1 atm at 1,234.93 K condensed and conversion at 961.78 °C. [1][2] This is a thermometer balancing silver solid and liquid, which is a fixed point of ITS-90; it describes pure element, not 925 silver, silver welding or a uniform melting of silver alloys. [1][2] Thermal, conductive and reflective properties of silver are often discussed, but the melting point answers only to case change and does not directly determine these functions.

How?

Silver fixes are achieved by controlled condensation: high-purity silver melts completely in an inert atmosphere, cools slowly and stabilizes the temperature on the condensed platform. The standard platinum-resistance temperature meter is inserted into a fixed-point container, after which the platform is recorded. [1][2] The fixed-point table for NIST thermostats lists the tables for silver 1,234.93 K, RSC element 961.78 °C, 1,234.93 K, which are cross-referenced. [1][2] impurities such as oxygen and copper in samples change the shape of the platform, so the experiment emphasizes purity, purity and temperature measurements.

Value and Measurement condition

1,234.93 K - 273.15 K = 961.78 °C. This conversion is accurate to 0.01 K, but it is a thermostat value, not a measured accuracy per silverware. 925 Other metals containing about 7.5% silver are usually melted in a section where the solid phase is less than pure silver; the melting of silver welds is dependent on the formulation. [2] When comparing silver, the purity, alloying and measurement conditions should be specified and the “melting point of silver” should not be reported.

Common misunderstandings

"The melting point of the silver may be used to identify the colour" does not work: the melting point only reflects the case change and the colour requires evidence of composition, density or imprint. The second misunderstanding is to put a silver fixed spot on 925 silver; both the solid and the liquid phase of the alloy are to be determined by composition. [2] Third, it is considered that silver has a melting point below gold that is easier to process and less comprehensive; processing windows are also affected by oxidation, defusing and intensity. Finally, the decimals of the thermostat value do not equal the precision of the jewellery detection.
