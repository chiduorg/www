Hot spring water temperature

Machine-assisted draft · native review pending

Most hot springs for human impregnation are 30-45 °C, whichever is 40 °C (313 K). It refers to water temperature in springs or baths, not thermal reservoir; differences between vents, ponds and post-temperature measurements must be explained.

What is it?

Temperatures usually refer to the temperature of water that is impregnated in springs and baths, rather than to thermal subsurface. The bathing guide of the Ministry of the Environment of Japan and the Japan Springs Association uses 42 °C as a high-temperature bath warning line; the bath water in the Blue Lake of Iceland is constantly at 37 - 40 °C. [3] [4] Taken together, most hot springs are 30-45 °C, which takes 40 °C (313 K). [2] The spring itself is a geothermal phenomenon, and the spring temperature is only a measurable parameter of its surface.

How?

The temperature of the spring can be measured by temperature in the vent or bath, but there are significant differences between the two: deep-sea hot water is mixed with shallow cold water and evaporated in the pond. An example from Iceland shows that geothermal water can reach 240 °C before power generation and is cooled to 37-40 °C before entering the bath. [4] Therefore, the “spattime” must indicate the point of detection; it is stated only that the ephemeral temperature of the geothermal reservoir is considered a bath. Measurement should also avoid the moment when hot or cold water is mixed and uneven.

Value and Measurement condition

313 K equals 39.85 °C, approximately 40 °C. Japanese data suggest that older persons, persons with hypertension or heart disease avoid high temperature baths exceeding 42 °C, which suggests that the vicinity of 40 °C is not “absolute comfort”, but rather a zone of control between length and individual conditions. [2][3] Dissolved salt, pH and gaseous components of hot springs also change body perception and skin reaction; under the same temperature, different springs can bring different tolerance. [1] The article therefore includes conditions for media, site and leaching.

Common misunderstandings

First, the hot springs are equated with “the hotter, the more effective”; the cardiovascular burden is significantly increased at 42 °C and above. Secondly, the springs are compared to temperature; the hot springs are more stable and the air is more affected by weather. Third, all hot springs are thought to be close to boiling; most baths are at 30-45 °C, and the openings that are really close to boiling point need to be removed or cooled. Fourth, oblivion time; Japan suggested about 3-10 minutes at a time, which could be extended after adaptation, rather than just temperature.

Relationship to other temperature

40 °C is close to 38 °C for ever, but one is a bathing environment, and the other is a human core; it is well below 80 °C for sauna and below 100 °C for boiling-water. Compared to approximately 350 °C of hydrothermal-vent, the hot springs are only the low-temperature end of the geothermal system after cooling the surface. Clear measurements, media and exposure time can avoid mixing “geo-hot water” with “bum bath water”.
