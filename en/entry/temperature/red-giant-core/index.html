Red giant core helium-burning temperature

Machine-assisted draft · native review pending

Helium combustion at the core of the Red Star is usually close to 10⁸ K. NASA's teaching materials write this threshold in about 100 million Kelvin, and NED's nuanced chapter gives the same order of magnitude; Benentry uses about 10⁸ K as the core reference, and shows that it is not the temperature of the entire star of the Red Big Star, nor is it a lightball effective temperature.

What is it?

The Red Star is a low-massive massstar phase in which core hydrogen is depleted and the outer layer becomes colder, while the core shrinks up. [1] The term temperature here is the modelteperature of the central area when helium burns, not the temperature of the entire red star; the light ball of the red star may be only a few thousand and the core may be close to 100 million. [1][2]

How?

The core is not directly accessible, but can only be calculated by the star structure and evolution model: given mass, composition and mix length, which gives the density and temperature required for the core to contract to helium combustion. The NASA teaching materials state that when core temperature reaches about 100 million Kelvin, helium is concentrated into carbon and released energy to temporarily support Star; the NED's nucleo-synthesis section also sets this threshold at about 100 million degrees. [1][2]

Value and Measurement condition

Here about 10⁸ K is used as a core reference and is not written as a precise threshold. For low massstar, the core may be in a simple state of Electron, and helium ignition occurs in the form of helium flash; for larger massstar, the conditions for ignition differ from the evolutionary path. [1][2] Therefore,10⁸ K is the “order of magniente threshold”, not all red stars are set on fire at the same time at the same time, let alone the average of the entire star.

Common misunderstandings

First, consider the entire star of the Red Star as 100 million; the outer light ball is actually colder, but the core is warming. Second, helium combustion is seen as a simple continuation of hydrogen combustion; fuel, product and energy release rates vary. Thirdly, it is assumed that all red stars will explode immediately; most low-massstars will enter horizontal branches, draw closer to giant branches, and the shell will form a white dwarf instead of a direct supernova. [2]

Relations with other temperate areas

10⁸ K is about 15 MK higher than the core of the Sun, and it is also over thousands of hours of red star light; it is below about 0.7 GK of carbon combustion and about 2 GK of oxygen combustion. Put it on the map of the hot zone, and both the "core" and the "light ball" must be marked, otherwise the low-temperature appearance of the Red Star will be confused with the 100 million-dollar core. [1][2]

The othermodynamicsteperature unit of this article is based on BIPM's definition for Kelvin. [3]

Solar core temperature
