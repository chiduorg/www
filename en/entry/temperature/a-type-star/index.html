A-type stellar temperature

Machine-assisted draft · native review pending

A type of star is a highly hydrogen-lined Star category. The teaching classification places the temperature area at approximately 7,500– 11,000 K, Benentry follows the scale anchor of approximately 9,500 K and maintains the previously recorded range of 7,500 - 10,000 K; it is not a real measure of a single star, nor is it a unified temperature of the whole star class.

What is it?

A The type of star is a class of Star in the spectra classification, characterized by a strong neutral hydrogen absorption line that is visible only at a later stage in the calcium ion subcategory. [1] Its "surface temperature" usually refers to a valid temperature, i.e. the conversion of the total irradiation from star to the temperature derived from the same radius in bold, which is not the same as the reading of a part of the light ball. Weasel is a common example. [2] The common denominator of type A is the strength of the hydrogen line, but the spectral detail of the subcategory from A0 to A9 varies continuously, indicating that the temperature gradient remains within the category. [1]

How?

The classification is based on a comparison of the star spectrum with the standard spectra to determine the spectra type; it is then combined with the vision, the interferometric diameter and the thermal radiation currents, and is designed to be effective attemperature with atmospheric model. [1] In the absence of a reliable angular diameter, thermal radiation flow can only be measured by giving surface fluxes, and temperature still relies on the model radius and polishing assumptions. [1] Teaching materials place the A type at about 7,500-11,000 K and give about 9,500 K a representative value. [1] Another material describes this type of object of magnitude with about 10,000 K. [2]

Value and Measurement condition

9,500 K is the scale anchor, corresponding to a temperature zone of approximately 7,500-10,000 K. This anchor is not a real measure of an A-type star, nor is it a unified temperature for the whole star class; A0 and A9 are thousands of kelvins different from the effective temperature, and there are 10,000 and 11,000 K for the category baseary. [1][3] Place 9,500 K in a modern Star sample, which is only an integral point in the middle of type A and is not a substitute for star-by-star integration. [3]

Common misunderstandings

First, the spectra is used as a precise temperature count; it varies from subcategory to subcategory within the same type. Second, effective temperature is considered to be comprehensible on the surface; rotation, convection and magnetic fields cause local area differences. Thirdly, seeing blue and white thinks that A is close to the hottest Star; it's far below the tens of thousands of Kelvins of the O. [3]
