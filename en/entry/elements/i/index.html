Why does it matter?

Iodine 5p5 is only one electron short of an ectron, which gives it a strong preference for an electron in chemical reactions, forming I-. Negative electricity 2.66 indicates that it attracts electrons more than thallium, but is weaker than bromine 2.96; the downward trend in negative power from top to bottom of the homologue continues here. [2] Iodine can form a double atom molecule or be combined with other non-metals by covalent; in the oxidation response, it is often reduced as an oxidizer to iodine ion. Iodine chemistry is more concentrated and closer to closed shells than “two less” aluminum.

More notable is the nuclear dimension: Iodine has only one stable isotope 127I. [2] This is related to its odd Z = 53, which usually does not favour the formation of large quantities of stable nuclides; by contrast, the next element Z is even Z, stabilizing isotope to nine at once. If Iodine is seen side by side with thorium, it is found that “chemically one Electron” and “nuclearly stable isotope surge” are right next to each other: the former is determined by 5p filling, and the latter by the number of protons and the neutron crust. Iodine is therefore a typical sample of halogen chemistry and a good example of a nuclear stability interpretation page.

Where did it come from?

Facts label the iodized nuclear source as "r process and s process". Iodine is not a product of iron-peak burning, but is produced gradually by heavy seed nuclei in the capture environment of Neutron: the process is slowly advancing along the stabilization line in the near-grasses, r the process is fast-growing at the peak of neutron fluxes combined with supernovas or neutrons, and then back to the stabilization line via beta decay. [1] Iodine is located further behind the iron peak, and the co-pressure of the colossals of the cologne and the neutron prisoners makes it far less abundant than iron and nickel; however, the overlap between the two paths still allows for a share of Iodine in heavy element.
