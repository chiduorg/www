Bacteria

Bacterium is a single cell nuclear organism that has no membrane package kernel; this page uses 1 micrometre as an introductory scale reference to indicate the overview range of 0.1-5.0 micrometres, morphological differences and measurement provision.

What's that?

Bacterium is a large group of single-cell nuclear organisms: they have no membrane kernels or membranes such as linear particles and green leaves, and genetic material is concentrated in the central nuclear zone of the quell, which contains nuclear sugar. [1][2] acterium has a wide variety of shapes, with a common spiral of spherical, rodic and bent; the same shape may also be linked to chains or clusters at different stages of division. [2]

Size Range and Measurement

OpenStax summarizes the diameter of the original nuclear cell at approximately 0.1-5.0 micrometres and compares it with the true core of approximately 10-100 micrometres in diameter. [1] For a particular species, the diameter, length or width must be specified: the diameter is commonly used in the fungus, and the length and width are often reported separately in the fungus; conditions of preparation, fixation and dyeing, micrometry also affect readings. The 1 micrometre on this page is the prime reference in this range and does not correspond to a specified strain. [1][2]

Why is it usually small?

When the cell approximates the sphere, surface area isa increases by radius square, and volume increases by radius cube. When the cell becomes larger, the membrane area corresponding to the unit of volume falls, and the amount of nutrient input and waste discharged by diffusion increases. [1] The original nuclecell maintains smaller sizes, which facilitate rapid diffusion and fragmentation, while the nucquell develops the quell skeleton, membrane system and the quell device to organize long-range transport. Size is not a “lower or advanced” label, but a scale that complements the organizational structure.

Common misunderstandings

“bacterium is a fungus of disease” does not work: in nature and in humans, there is a large number of bacteriums, most of which are not ill, and many are involved in nutrient cycling, food fermentation and host symbiosis. [2] “1 μm is the standard diameter of the bacterium” is equally non-accuracy: different groups, different morphology and different growth patterns vary considerably. The virus is usually more than one order of magnitude smaller than the bacterium, but the virus is not a cell and cannot be compared at the same structural level.
