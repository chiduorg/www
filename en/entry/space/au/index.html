Astronomical Unit

Machine-assisted draft · native review pending

Astrónomical unit is now qualified as a precise 149,597,870,700 metres for expression of the inner distance of the solar system; it is not equal to the solar-terrestrial distance at every moment.

What is it?

Astromonical unit (au) is a commonly used unit of length of distance within the solar system. In 2012, the International Astronomical Union fixed its value at 149,597,870,700 metres, or approximately 149.6 million kilometres. [1][2] It is no longer an instantaneous distance from Earth to the Sun, but an agreed value that can be understood as the modern calibration of the semi-long axis of Earth's orbit. [1]

How?

This definition came from a multi-wheel measurement of historical vision, Venus Leng Day and radar ranging, which was finally fixed by IAU resolution in 2012. [1][2] JPL gives 1 au along with light speed, and Julian year, to facilitate the passage of astronomical distance into light: 1 au equals approximately 499.005 light seconds. [2] Once fixed, the unit no longer has its own measurementor, but the orbital decomposition of a specific object is still with error. Early measurements of solar vision using Venus Lent Day, with a shift to radar ranging in the twentieth century and a gradual increase in accuracy; after the 2012 fixation, observation focused on planetary calendars.

Value and Measurement condition

This entry records the unit definition value, so application is false. [1] The physical distance of the Earth from the Sun continues to change with the public: NASA gives an average of about 149.6 million kilometres, approximately 147.1 million kilometres in recent days and about 152.1 million kilometres in distant days. [3] These three numbers refer to instant or average solar-terrestrial distances, not the unit of “1 au”.

Common misunderstandings

The common misconception is that “astromical unit is the present solar-terrestrial distance” or that the Earth is larger when it becomes remote from the Sun. The former mixes units with instantaneous measration, while the latter reverses the value of definiation and observation. [3] Another misunderstanding is to use au as an angle unit; it is a unit of length and cannot be directly interchangeable with angular seconds and parsec. Mixing aau with light-year is also a common mistake: one light-year equals about 63,241 au, four more times of magnitude.

Relationship with other scale

On the scale axis, 1 au equals about half a long axis of Earth orbit and about 8.3 centimeters. Neptune's orbit is about 30 au, its main belt is about 30 to 55 au, and its outer edge is about 100,000 au. [2] It is therefore appropriate to describe lines of galaxy and to describe Star distance more easily with light-year or parsec.
