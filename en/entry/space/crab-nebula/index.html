What is it?

The Crab Nebula is a relic of the supernova of 1054 and is located in the Taurus, about 6,500 light-years from Earth. [1][2] It consists of a gaseous filament structure, a synchronized radiation luminous zone and a central pulsar, and is the classic object of research into supernovae relics and pulsed wind nebula. [1] The filament structure of the nebula is the product of supernovae ejections interacting with surrounding media.

How?

Astronomers in China and elsewhere recorded the appearance of guest stars in July 1054, with 23 days of daylight; this corresponds to the position of the nebula and the rate of expansion. [1] Modern observation still expands at about 1,100 kilometres per second, with a 30-second pulse star at the centre providing energy to the nebula. [1] Hubble's full Marseek image gives filament structure details. [2] Multiband observation combines radio, infrared, visible, X-ray and gamma-ray data on the same object.

Common misunderstandings

The common misconception is to mix the “10light-year” of the crab nebula with its distance of 6,500 light-years; the difference is about 650 times. Another misconception is that supernova sites continue to expand indefinitely at the same speed; they actually interact with the interstellar medium to slow down and transform. [1]
