Ultraviolet

Ultraviolet rays are electromagnetic bands outside the visible ultraviolet end and before X-rays, with a wavelength of about 400 to 10 nm. This article takes 100 nm as the wavelength representing a vacuum.

What is it?

Ultraviolet light is an electromagnetic band outside the visible ultraviolet end, before the X-ray, which is invisible to human eyes. [1] The volume of this article is the wavelength in a vacuum, not the intensity or the degree of sunburning. Ultraviolet rays are absorbed by oxygen and ozone in the air, so short-wave ultraviolet is weak on the ground and astronomical observation is often required outside the atmosphere.

How?

Britannica writes about the wavelengths of ultraviolet radiation in the range of 400 to 10 nm, indicating that part of the information extends the short wave end to 4 nm; it also divides by biological effect into UVA 400 to 315 nm, UVB 315 to 280 nm, and UVC 280 to 100 nm. Common misunderstandings

It is not comprehensive to consider UV only as tan light: UVB is more directly related to DNA damage, and UVC is often blocked by the ozone layer. Another misconception is that the ultraviolet index is considered wavelength; it is a weighted strength indicator. Nor can it be assumed that all UV rays reach the ground, and most short-wave UVs are absorbed at high altitude. [1]

Relationship with other scale

The 100 nanometres are about a quarter of the minimum wavelength of 400 nanometres of visible light and 100 times longer than the one nanometre commonly used for X-rays. [1][2] Ultraviolet light is located between visible light and X-ray, is the main spectra of the Electron leap in the outer layer of atom and is the core wave band of ozone layer formation and protection. It is both a means of sunburning and disinfecting and an important window for space astronomy. Ultraviolet observation in the atmosphere avoids water vapour and ozone absorption, and industrial solidification and fluorescent stimulation select light sources by band.
