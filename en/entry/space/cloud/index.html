Cloud

Machine-assisted draft · native review pending

This article uses approximately 1 km of scale, for example, a typical cloud. It is the assumption in meteorological teaching that clouds are approximately 1 km of cubes, and is not a uniform size of cloud height, cloud thickness or all cloud types.

What is it?

Clouds are water droplets or crystal clusters suspended in the atmosphere. The "horizontal" of clouds measured in this Article is the approximate width from one side to the other, not the height of the cloud, the thickness of the cloud or the amount of clouds. [1][2] Clouds are not rigid, and the edges are determined by water vapour condensation and evaporation, so scale can only be approximated. The scale of different clouds is very different, and the cloud is only one example that can be easily compared.

How?

The National Meteorological Administration of the United States, in explaining the “mixed cloud”, has given a 1-kilometre level of scale to a cube that resembles a typical cloud of clear skies of approximately 1 km x 1 km x 1 km. [1] NOAA indicates that clouds are extremely large in shape and size and can be flat and light, indicating that a single number does not cover all clouds. [2] These values are the model assumptions used for teaching and estimation, and are not precise for a cloud.

Value and Measurement condition

1 km equals 1,000 m. It describes the horizontal width of the cloud and is often used as a reference for its vertical thickness, but its substrate height can be up to a few kilometres from the near ground. [1][2] Equally cloudy, rainy clouds can scale up to tens of kilometres and layers of clouds can spread hundreds of kilometres, so that one kilometre cannot be considered the diameter of the whole cloud.

Common misunderstandings

"The size of the cloud is its width on the map" is incomplete: the cloud will be shaped, merged or dispersed with the wind, and there is no fixed position in the baseary. [1][2] Another misunderstanding is that cloud levels scale are considered below cloud heights; the two are not in the same direction, and units are not interchangeable, although they use metres. When comparing clouds, the cloud type, timing and measurement approach should be described.

Relationship with other scale

One kilometre is approximately 9.5 times the length of 105 metres for a football field and about 11 per cent of Everest heights. [1][2] A cloud is small compared to the diameter of a hurricane of hundreds of kilometres; but it is a million times larger than the diameter of the raindrop millimetre class. Clouds are in the “100 to 10 km” weather scale, which links up storms and planetary circulation.

This article uses SI metre-based length units, following the BIPM definition of the metre. [3]

Based on BIPM definition's vacuum light speed of 299,792,458 m/s, it takes about 3.3 microseconds for light to pass through this length, order of magnitude on the time axis. [3]
