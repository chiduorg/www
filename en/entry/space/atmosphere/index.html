Atmosphere of Earth

Machine-assisted draft · native review pending

The Earth's atmosphere has no sudden end to physics. This article uses the agreed altitude of 100 km from the sea level to the Carmen Line as a valid thickness, not the total atmospheric altitude or the flying altitude of a particular molecule.

What is it?

The Earth's atmosphere is a gas layer that surrounds the Earth, with a rapid decrease in density with altitude and without a physical “end”. The volume of this article is the effective thickness of the sea level to the Carmen Line, taking 100 kilometres as a comparison of the level of the atmosphere; it is not the entire altitude of the atmosphere or the flying altitude of a particular molecule. [1][2]

How?

The atmospheric stratification is determined by temperaturee, composition and density. NOAA places the lower thermal layer at about 85 kilometres, extending it to about 600 kilometres and extending it to about 10,000 kilometres. [3] The Carmen Line is an agreement for engineering and record keeping: 100 km for FAI and at times 80 km for NASA and the United States Air Force. [1][2] These altitudes come from rockets, satellites, radars and sounding machines and are not a line of direct contact.

Value and Measurement condition

This article uses 100 kilometres, or 100,000 metres, as it is the Carmen Line common to international air records; Britannica also reminds that 80 to 100 kilometres are used. [1][2] 100 km is not the upper limit of atmospheric mass: most atmosphere mass is concentrated at lower levels, but thin gases continue to extend to hundreds or even thousands of kilometres. [3] Therefore, the “gauge” is the agreed height of the baseary, not the geometry of the atmosphere.

Common misunderstandings

“There is no air beyond 100 kilometres” is wrong: there is very thin air, but the satellite still encounters resistance and needs orbit maintenance. The Carmen Line is also not considered legal; it is an agreement between works and records, with different institutions using 80 or 100 kilometres. [1][2] Another misunderstanding is that by dividing the thickness of the atmosphere directly by the height of Everest, the result is that there are only a few Everest peaks in the atmosphere; this comparison ignores the decrease in density with the height index.

Relationship with other scale

The 100 km range is approximately 11.3 times the altitude of 8,848.86 metres at Everest, 1.6 per cent of the Earth ' s average radius of 6,371 kilometres and about a quarter of the orbit altitude of approximately 400 kilometres at the international space station. [1][3] Compared to the Earth's diameter of 12,742 kilometres, 100 kilometres are a thin layer, but already contain the vast majority of weather and aircraft routes.
