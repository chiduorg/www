Sun

Machine-assisted draft · native review pending

This page uses the solar diameter of about 1,391,400 km, which is obtained by multiplying the average radius of 695,700 km in the NASA fact sheet by 2. The radiance and corona around the Sun are excluded from this comparative value.

A Star without a hard surface.

The Sun consists mainly of hydrogen and helium and maintains its overall structure with its own gravity. It is not a ball with a hard crust like the Earth; what is known daily as the “surface of the sun” usually refers to a light ball with a large amount of visible light. Nuclear fusion in the inner core of the Sun provides energy, and the core, the light ball and the outer atmosphere belong to different regions and do not share a temperature. [2]

What's the diameter of this page?

This page uses twice the average radius of 695,700 km in the NASA Factual Table and receives 1,391,400 km of reference diameter. The size comparison uses this commonly used Sun-visible metallion convulsion to exclude the luminous, ejective structure and outward extension of the corona from the same path. Adding greater light to the sun does not mean that its relative diameter increases with animation. [1][2]

Why make a distinction between length, volume and mass?

The Sun ' s diameter is approximately 109 times greater than the Earth ' s average radius in the fact sheet. If the Earth's diameter is reduced to 20 centimetres, the solar diameter is about 21.8 metres. 109 is the ratio of length, not mass; for ideal balls, volume changes three times in diameter, and mass depends on density. Therefore, the term “109 times width” cannot be directly described as “109 times weight”. [1]

The error book of this value.

The Sun is approximately 1.392 million kilometres in diameter and is commonly rounded “about 1.4 million kilometres”. It is extrapolated from the angle diameter to the solar-terrestrial range, and there is no hard-bundary on the edge of the light ball, so different definition can be tens of kilometres away. The sun is not a perfect sphere, and the rotation and magnetic field causes a small flat rate. When the sun is loaded into a cube, the edge is measured in diameter rather than in volume.

Another scale to look at it.

The Sun is about 109 times the diameter of the Earth; if the Earth is 1 cm, the Sun is about 1.09 m. The average distance from the Sun is about 150 million kilometres, equivalent to 107 solar diameters. This ratio suggests that the sun looks big because it's close to us; in the world of Star, the sun is only a small primary sequence.

Will the size of the sun change?

The sun is not a fixed ball. In the main sequence, it uses hydrogen as its core fuel and the radius changes slowly; when it enters the Red Giantula stage, the outer layer expands significantly. Today's 1,391,400 kilometres is just the current lightball reference diameter, not the fixed size of the sun's lifetime. [2] When the sun is put into Star evolution, the length axis records “how big is now” and thetime axis records “where it is”. The same object, the two axes answer different questions.

The solar radius is changing.

The solar radius is usually 696,000 kilometres, but the light ball is not hard-bound, and the radius depends on the light ball temperature or optical thickness. The continued loss of mass and radiation in the Sun is theoretically slow to shrink; changes in observation are influenced by the activity cycle and instrumentation calibration. Using the solar radius as a fixed constant is the approximation of the project and not the end point of Star physics. [1][S1]
