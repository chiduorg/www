Visible Light

Machine-assisted draft · native review pending

Visible light is an electromagnetic wave narrowband that can be seen in the eyes, with a wavelength of about 400 to 700 nm. Quantity in this article takes 550 nm as the median range, which is the wavelength range in the vacuum.

What is it?

Visible light is a narrow zone in the electromagnetic spectrum that can be seen in the eye, between infrared and ultraviolet. [1] The volume in this article is a wavelength range, not a single wavelength; the vacuum differs very little from the value in air. The "colour" of the visible light is determined by the wavelength and the eye response, and the wavelength itself does not determine the colour alone.

How?

Britannica gives visible light from about 700 nm red to 400 nm purple light. [1] NASA's spectra lists give a range of 400 to 700 nm, with a frequency of approximately ZXQQPH0QZX to 7.5×10¹⁴ H, and photoenergy of approximately 3×10⁻¹⁹ to 5×10⁻¹⁹ J. [2][3] These ranges are common approximations, not sharp divisions; perceptions of endpoints vary. The colour description of the camera and monitor is often borrowed from the range, but the actual response of the device is not an ideal rectangle.

If single-colour light is discussed, specific wavelengths should be given; if white light is discussed, the full range should be indicated. The medium refraction changes the wavelength within the medium, but the frequency remains unchanged. [3]

Common misunderstandings

Visible light as “no-hazardous radiation” is not comprehensive: strong light can damage the eyes, and ultraviolet light energy is higher. [3] Another misconception is that wavelengths are fixed to colours; the same colour can also be produced from different spectral combinations. Nor can the visible light range be considered the entire electromagnetic spectrum, which is a narrow segment. [2]
