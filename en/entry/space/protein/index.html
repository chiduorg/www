Protein Molecule

Machine-assisted draft · native review pending

Protein is a large molecule that folds into a three-dimensional structure with a wide variety of shapes and sizes; this page refers to approximately 5.5 nm of haemoglobin as indicating the scale relationship between the protein and folding.

What's a protein?

Protein is a large molecule that connects amino acids to growth chains and folds into specific three-dimensional structures, and is involved in almost all life processes, such as catalytic, structural support, transport, signals and immunization. [1][2] The amino acid sequence determines the type of protein; different sequences can also vary in folding, shape and function. Protein has species-specific and tissue-specific characteristics, and proteins vary from species to organ. [2]

Dimensions and Measurement

This page uses haemoglobin as a specific example: NIH's nanoscale describes the oxygen-borne protein in the red blood cell as about 5.5 nm wide. This is not the average diameter of all proteins. Protein shapes vary widely: some are near-spherical, some are long and some form an inner fibre or membrane spiral; the "width" of the measurement must indicate direction, assembly state and whether it contains a sugar chain or a supporting foundation. [1][2] Hemoglobins consist of several Archipelagics, and the individual Archipelagic and polypolymeric shapes are different.

Why are they so diverse?

Protein function relies on a three-dimensional structure. The amino acid chain is folded by hydrogen, hydrophobic, ion and disulphur keys; error folding may result in functional loss or accumulation. [2] Some proteins need to be folded with partner proteins, and some form a membrane spiral. Structure determines function, but structure is not static: combination of substrates, change of temperaturee or phosphoric acidification all may cause structural change. [2]

Common misunderstandings

"Protein is a straight line" is wrong: the amino acid chain folds into a three- and four-stage structure, with shape and function depending on the folding. [2] “5.5 nm is the width of all proteins” is also incorrect: haemoglobin is only an example chosen for this page and the proteins vary considerably. In addition, proteins are not equivalent to “muscle protein”; enzymes, antibodies, transport proteins and structural proteins are all proteins.

Relationship with other scale

The erythroprotein of 5.5 nm is in nanoscale, much smaller than a typical cell (about 20 μm); it is made of multiple amino acid chains and is larger than a single amino acid molecule. [1] A red blood cell contains a large number of hemoglobin molecules; from molecule to cell, scale crossed three orders of magnitude.

The metre-based length units of this article are based on BIPM for metres definition. [3]
