International Space Station

Machine-assisted draft · native review pending

NASA registered 310 feet (94 metres) of shelf length at the International Space Station. This article uses the length of the shelf and indicates that 109 metres is the total span of the solar wing, not the shelf itself.

What is it?

The international space station is a multinational cooperative manned spacecraft operating in low-Earth orbit, consisting of a pressurized segment, an integrated frame structure, a solar wing, a radiator and an external payload. The article compares the length of the "silver frame" with the extent of the station's main skeleton along its long axis, not the total length of the solar wing, the length of the pressurized module or the total end of the station. [1] The accelerator is a stress structure for the installation of solar wings, heaters and external experimental equipment.

How?

The NASA “Station Acts” page is listed in the size and mass table as follows: a shelf length of 310 feet (94 metres), a pressurized chamber length of 218 feet (67 metres) along the main axis, a total of 239 feet (73 metres), mass 419,725 kg. [1] The same page is also known as Sun Wing Exhaustion 356 feet (109 metres). The NASA integrated frame page states that the frame structure reaches 108.5 metres (356 feet) after the solar wing stretches. [2] Both points to the same wing span.

Value and Measurement condition

The length of the frame is 94 metres. [1] 356 feet (109 metres) are the total width of the solar wing after its spread, which includes the solar wing and not just the turbo; 108.5 metres differ from 109 metres by the same height. [1][2] The length of the pressurized module 67 meters is another amount. The three figures answer “how long the skeleton” and “how wide the wing” “how long” and cannot replace one another. The structure must be specified.

Common misunderstandings

Using 109 meters as long as a turbo length, the breadth of the solar wing is factored into the skeleton. [2] Conversely, the use of 94 metres as the head of the space station is not acceptable, as the pressurized module and the docking spacecraft also extend outward. Another misunderstanding is the separation of 419,725 kg of mass from length to "line density"; the space station mass is very unevenly distributed, and this division has no physical significance.
