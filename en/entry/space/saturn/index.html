Saturn

Machine-assisted draft · native review pending

The average diameter of this page is approximately 116,464 km using the volume equivalent of Saturn's main subject. This value is based on an average radius of 58,232 km in the NASA fact sheet and is neither an equatorial diameter nor contains Saturn rings.

What is it?

Saturn is the second largest planet in the solar system and a gas giant with rings. This corresponds to the volume equivalent mean diameter of Saturn's subject, and does not contain Saturn rings. [1] It does not have a solid surface, and the radius is usually a bar-pressure layer definition; the stripes visible on the clouds are only the upper atmosphere. Saturn rotates about 10.7 hours a week, with low density and more equatorial than Jupiter. The main component of the ring is water ice, which is thin, but not included in this diameter.

How?

The NASA Factual List shows an average radius of 58,232 km, an equatorial radius of 60,268 km and a polar radius of 54,364 km; the average diameter of 58,232 doubles to 116,464 km. [1][2] The data are derived from flying images, star shelter, radio tracking and internal model. The Cassini has long surrounded Saturn, supplementing information on ring structures, cloud motion and gravity field. Britannica's overview of Saturn is compatible with the average radius adopted for this article.

Value and Measurement condition

116,464 kilometres are the main average diameter; the equatorial diameter is about 120,536 kilometres and the polar diameter is about 108,728. Saturn flattens 0.098, with a different range of ring systems. [1][2] The ring extends far from the top of the cloud, but the horizontal scope of the ring is not equal to the diameter of the planetary body. The average diameter, the equatorial diameter, the polar diameter and the total extent of the ring are four different measurement conventions; the notification should state whether the ring is contained.

Common misunderstandings

The term “Sat's diameter includes rings” is not valid: this article does not expressly contain rings; they can span hundreds of thousands of kilometres. Nor can the mean diameter be considered as the equatorial diameter. [1][2] Another misunderstanding is that the ring is a solid disk; it consists of a large number of ice particles and rock fragments, each operating in orbit. The average density of Saturn is about 687 kg per cubic metre, which is less than water, but this is the overall average and does not mean that it consists of liquid water.

Relationship with other scale

116,464 kilometres are about 9.14 times the Earth ' s diameter and about 83 per cent of Jupiter ' s average diameter. Saturn is about 8.4 per cent of the approximately 1.39 million kilometres in diameter of the Sun. It has an average density of 687 kg per cubic metre, which is lower than water. [1][2]

This article uses SI metre-based length units, following the BIPM definition of the metre. [3]
