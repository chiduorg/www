Supernova Remnant

Machine-assisted draft · native review pending

Supernova remains are fragments that swelled upwards after the Big Massstar explosion and luminous crust formations that sweep through the interstellar medium. Sampled by a crab nebula, it's about 10 light-years, about 9.5×10¹⁶ metres in diameter.

What is it?

Supernova remains are fragments that swelled in the wake of the Big Massstar explosion, as well as luminous crust formations by interstellar media that have been swept by shock waves and heat. [1][3] This page is sampled by a crab nebula measuring the diameter of the visible crust of the remnant, about 10 light-years; it is not the size of an explosive flash fireball or the diameter of the central neutron.

How?

The size of the supernova remains can be obtained from an angle diameter and distance, and the rate of expansion is matched by a spectra doppler-position shift or cross-geological view image-to-measurement. Britannica records that the Crab Nebula is a relic of a supernova of 1054, approximately 6,500 light-years, approximately 10 light-years in diameter, currently expanding at about 1,100 km/s. [2] The evolution of relics usually follows the stages of free expansion, heat insulation and radiation cooling, integration into interstellar media; the early shell radius can be about 10 light-years. [1] NASA indicates that after the collapse of the Big Mass core, the material dropped becomes a relic, and the centre may leave a neutron or black hole. [3]

Value and Measurement condition

10 light-years is about 9.5×10¹⁶ m2. [2] This value describes the visible crust of the crab nebula, not the fixed size of all supernova remains. There may be only a few light-years of young relics that can be extended to dozens or even hundreds of light-years; the same relics are not identical in different bands (radiation, X-ray, optical). [1][3] The “diameter” must therefore describe the sample, the band and the method.

Common misunderstandings

A common misconception is that supernova remains as a “fireball that still explodes”; the explosion itself lasts only seconds to minutes, and remains a structure that continues to expand for hundreds to tens of thousands of years. [1] Another misconception is to confuse the relics with the planetary nebula: the former from the core collapse of the large massstar or thermal explosion of the white dwarf star, and the latter from the lower massstar ejection. [1][3] There are also many differences in the size of the relic diameter as the central neutron star or black hole.

Relationship with other scale

9.5×10¹⁶ is about 10 light-years, about 2.4 times the distance from the Sun to the nearby star and five times the typical diameter of the planetary nebula. [1][2] It is thousands of times larger than the solar system's heliosphere, but only about one in 10,000 within the diameter of the galaxy. By putting supernova remains in the interstellar medium, you can see how a single explosion injects heavy element and energy into galaxy and triggers the next star formation.
