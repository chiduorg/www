Earth

Machine-assisted draft · native review pending

The Earth looks like a ball, but there is no “Earth radius” that applies to all questions. This page uses the volume equivalent mean diameter, combined with equatorial, bipolar and surface fluctuations, to illustrate the difference between object scale and geometry.

Let's get this straight.

“Earth diameter 12,742 kilometres” is an agreed average. The Earth rotates it slightly along the equator, and mountains, trenches, ice sheets and tidal waves divert the real surface from the smooth ellipse. NOAA has integrated work on the size, shape, gravity field and location of the Earth's surface into the Earth's measurement; therefore, “how big the Earth” is not the back of a number in the first place, but a set of scenarios. [2]

What are the answers to the three radiuss?

The NASA fact sheet gives an average radius of volume equivalents 6,371 km, equatorial radius 6,378.137 km, polar radius 6,356.752 km. [1] This page takes twice the average radius and gets 12,742 kilometres. It indicates that “the size of a ball identical to the Earth's volume” is not the distance that can be obtained from any direction through the Earth's heart. The equatorial diameter is about 12,756 kilometres and the polar diameter is about 12,714 kilometres; all three numbers are correct, but the problem is different.

The flat rate is not measurementor.

The equatorial diameter differs from the polar diameter by about 42-43 kilometres, compared to 12,742 kilometres by only about 0.33 per cent. This difference comes from the flat rate of rotation, which is part of the Earth's shape, not from the broken instrument. [1] When global averages, orbit estimates or comparisons of magnitude are made, average balls can be used; when using satellite positioning, gravity field or longitude calculations, reference ellipses and geodesic surfaces cannot be replaced by an integer. [2]

Why do you use average diameter?

The equatorial diameter is larger and the polar diameter is smaller. If only the equatorial diameter is reported, the global volume comparison and orbital scale will be high; only the extreme diameter will be reported and it will be low. The average ball uses volume as a common scale, which facilitates comparison with Moon, other planets and the Sun, and allows for a stable denominator “the Earth's diameter is several times that of Moon”. However, it is not suitable for precision positioning: navigation, gravity field and satellite orbit require reference ellipses rather than an average ball. The same Earth, with different kinds of questions.

From local baselines to satellite geodesy

The ground-based measurement starts with an accessible baseline, with angles and astronomical astronomy extrapolating larger arc lengths; modern-day geo-measurement puts satellite altimeters, global positioning, gravity-based and Earth-rotation parameters into a global framework. [1][2] A "radio" given in different frameworks may be an average ball, reference ellipse or local geoid. When you read the Earth's radius, you ask which side it belongs to. It's more useful than remembering more decimals.

Mass and gravity: another "Earth Size"

The same fact sheet also gave the Earth mass approximately 5.97×10²⁴ kg. [1] Mass, together with the radius, determines surface gravity and the atmospheric marker and satellite orbit. Mass is not “volume multiplied by a density” as simple: the Earth's inner density varies with depth and the earth's cores, mantles and crust vary. The length axle says "how big" and the mass axle says "how many" and the two cannot be replaced.

Five-axis Contrast

On the length axis, the Earth is a10⁷-mite class planet; on the mass axis, it is a ZXXQPH1QX-kg class rock planet; on thetime axis, it records the evolution of about 4.5 billion years; and on the temperature axis, there are different temperature zones in the sea surface, atmosphere and interior. Dissociate them in order to avoid the confusion between “larger Earth equals weight” or “sea diameter of Earth is distance to the Earth's core”. The five axes describe the same object, but each answers different questions; cross-axis reading does not rank numbers in parallel, but in parallel.
