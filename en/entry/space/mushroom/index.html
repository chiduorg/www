Mushroom

Machine-assisted draft · native review pending

Mushrooms are visible sub-entities of some fungi, not plants. This article uses as a reference the common umbrella bacterium cap of 0.10 m (10 cm) in diameter, measured at the widest line distance and not at the height of the whole strain, the length of the bacterium or the extent of the fungus.

What is it?

Mushrooms are visible sub-entities of certain fungi, which perform the function of generating and releasing spores; they consist of parts such as bacterium caps, bacterium handles and bacterium filaments, and underground. A mushroom is not a plant and there is no chlorophyll. The article compares the “bacterial lid diameter”: the widest part of the lid is the straight distance through the centre, not the entire height, nor the length of the bacterium.

How?

Britannica's mushromentry uses mushrooms as umbrella spores for certain fungi, and indicates that the baptism covers the top structure that supports the bacterium. [1] The same station gives a more specific dimension: the bacterium cap is about 4-16 cm in diameter and the bacterium handle is 4-18 cm in length. [2] Ten centimetres fall within this fungus and are therefore used as a medium reference for common umbrella bacteria, but it is not the average of the species or a measurement for all mushrooms.

Value and Measurement condition

0.10 m equals 10 cm and 100 mm. When a quantum cap is in its diameter, it is taken at its widest and passes through the centre; if the amount is approximately 31.4 centimetres in diameter, the two cannot be mixed. [2] The fungus expands from hemispheric to flat shape with maturity, and the projection diameter changes; the length of the bacterium is another amount in the vertical direction. Ten centimetres is the whole value and does not mean that the measurement is precise to millimetres.

Common misunderstandings

The common misconception is that mushrooms are treated as plants. The mushrooms are fungus, absorbing organic matter from myceloids without photocooperative use. [1] There is also a misunderstanding about the diameter of the bacterium cap as a whole height: there may also be a few centimetres to a dozen centimetres of bacterium cover under a 10-cm bacterium baptism cover, the whole height of which is not equal to the bacterium baptism diameter. [2] Nor can all mushrooms be described in 10 centimetres: Britannica mentions that some mushroom fungus cover can be 20-25 centimetres, spherical fungi can be up to 1.5 metre, and microfuses need magnifying lenses to be seen. The size of the fungus cannot be used to determine if it is edible.

Relationship with other scale

The 10 centimetres are about the palm width of adults, about one tenth of the 1 metre high aluminum, and about 50,000 micrometres of intestinal bacterium growing up in length. [1][2] On the scale axis, the fungus cover is in the “centimetre to ten centimetres” of everyday biological belts: up, the aluminum, the oak tree and the sprout, down, the leafcell, the bacterium and the virus. Compare mushrooms, basketballs and eyeballs with the same "diameter" comparison to avoid mixing diameter, perimeter and altitude.

This article uses SI metre-based length units, following the BIPM definition of the metre. [3]
