Gerald R. Ford-class aircraft carrier

The Ford (CVN-78) has a full load of approximately 100,000 metric tons, i.e. approximately 1.0×10⁸ kg, and 1QX kg at 1 metric tons = 1,016 kg conversion. This is a ship-wide mass of aviation fuel and ammunition, not a standard discharge.

What's this reading?

Gerald R. Ford, the United States Navy's Ford class flagship, has a capacity of approximately 100,000 long tons, or approximately 1.0×10⁸ kg. [1] Captain 337 m, wide flight deck 78 m, served in 2017, driving four axes with two A1B pressurized water piles. The full load of discharges refers to the total mass when the vessel, equipment, aircraft on board, aviation fuel and ammunition are fully in place, and also to the state of water consumption at its worst.

Measureconvention with uncertainty

The Navy customarily reported in long tons, one long ton equals 1,016 kg and weighs approximately 1.6 per cent of the metric tons, so that 100,000 long ton conversion is followed by 1.02×10⁸ kg. The other one that must be separated is the standard discharge: it does not contain fuel, ski oil and spare boiler water, and the value is lower. Which one of these measures determines the location of the ship on the mass axis. This article takes a uniform full load discharge and specifies long tons of conversion to avoid using10⁸ kg as a precise place reading.

How?

The shipyard weighs in fractions at the stage between the docks and the docks, and then works on a tilt test to get a gravitational weight and then swaps water with a static curve for drainage volume. [2] Nuclear-powered ships do not burn fuel, but aviation fuel still accounts for a large portion of the fuel, and aircraft and ammunition on board are counted as fully loaded. The final release is a value that is accounted for by design condition and that is derived mainly from the condition definition rather than the precision of the scales.

What's a full load?

The 100 million tons of the Ford are full-load discharges: total mass when the vessel, equipment, aircraft on board, aviation fuel and ammunition are fully in place. Standard discharges do not include fuel, ski oil and spare boiler water, and are lower. Nuclear power does not burn fuel for naval use, but aviation fuel remains a full-load component. [1][S2]

The full-load discharge of the carrier also changes with the condition of the vehicle: when performing different tasks, the fleet of aircraft on board, ammunition and aviation fuel are different, and the amount of water consumed and discharged changes accordingly. One hundred thousand tons are designed to be full and not measured at each departure.

The carrier mass is also able to obtain drainage by volume conversion: the water density of volume, which is filled with water consumption; and the tilt test to give a weight position.

Nuclear power doesn't burn fuel, but aviation fuel is still heavy.

The Ford class nuclear reactor provides power and does not require naval fuel, but the aviation fuel used on board aircraft remains part of the full-load discharge. [1] A single deployment of aviation fuel can be carried to thousands of tons, which directly affects water consumption and supply cycles. Understanding “nuclear power” as “no fuel at all” would result in the leakage of aviation fuel.

Read-ment convention and conversion

The volume used in this section is 1.0×10⁸ kg, full load discharge: about 100,000 long tons (approximately ZXXPH1QX kg) of ships, equipment, aviation fuel and ammunition are published by the United States Navy. Mass is wide and readings depend on wet/dry weight, blood/free of blood, package/full load, etc.; sample and measration conditions must be together. When comparing this reading to the adjacententry, replace it with the same unit and then take the margin; do not reduce it directly with a different value.
