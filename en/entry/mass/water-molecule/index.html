One water molecule consists of two hydrotoms and one oxygen atom, and the molecular mass 18.0153 u, combined with 0QX kg. Its mass is slightly smaller than the sum of three isolated atoms, and the difference is the molecular combination capacity.

What's this reading?

A water molecule consisting of two hydrogenatoms and one oxygenatom, chemical H2O. Pressed by IUPAC atomic weight, about 1.008 u, approximately 15.999 u, combined with 18.0153 u; multiplied by atomic mass constant1.66053907×10⁻²⁷ kg/u, received 2.9915×10⁻²⁶ ZX kg. [1][2] This is a single molecular mass, not a mole's water 18.0153 grams.

This is slightly smaller than the sum of the mass of two hydrogenatoms plus one oxygen atom, with a difference of about a few eV/c2, from chemical bonding. The mass axis describes it as an integral mass of molecules, rather than a direct addition of three atomic mass: a combined loss, although it is only a fraction of a billion, must be made clear. [2] The ice, liquid water and water vapour use the same molecular mass, and the case change is the inter-molecular arrangement, not the molecule itself.

Measureconvention with uncertainty

Here, 18.0153 u is conventional weight, which is averaged by the isotope abundance of the Earth sample; in the case of pure 1H216O, molecular mass will fall to 18.0106 u, which is about 0.026 per cent lower than the average. [1] This article therefore records “conventional water” and not one type of isotope. In contrast to 10⁻⁵ order of magnitude, uncertainty is much smaller than the natural fluctuations between different sources of water.
